An Officer He Photographed

Not one of Allen’s own decorations, but a record worth setting beside them: the man whose field modification rebuilt the aeroplanes Allen kept the radios in.

“Pappy” Gunn, and His Own Decorations

Paul Irvin Gunn (18 October 1899 – 11 October 1957) was the most improbable officer in the Southwest Pacific and, for Allen’s squadron, the most consequential. He had served twenty-one years in the Navy, retiring as a chief petty officer in December 1939 after instructing at Pensacola, and was a founder’s partner in Philippine Air Lines when the Philippines fell — leaving his wife and four children interned at Santo Tomas, which is generally held to explain a good deal about how hard he fought the rest of the war. Commissioned straight into the Army Air Forces and attached to the 3rd Bomb Group, he worked with Jack Fox, North American Aviation’s field representative at Charters Towers, on the modification that turned the B-25 into a low-level strafer: eight fixed .50-calibre guns firing forward, four of them through the old bomb-aiming panel. That is precisely the aeroplane Allen describes being handed. Gunn flew B-25 #112498 on the Royce Mission to the Philippines on 11 April 1942, and General Kenney later took him onto his personal staff as his special-projects man.

He finished the war a colonel, holding the Silver Star, two Distinguished Flying Crosses, the Legion of Merit, the Air Medal, the World War II Victory Medal — and nine Purple Hearts. He was killed on 11 October 1957 when his aircraft went down in a storm over the Philippines.

Sources: Paul Irvin “Pappy” Gunn in Australia during WW2, and 3rd Bombardment Group, Australia @ War (ozatwar.com); B-25 Strafer Field Modifications, Australia @ War; Pappy Gunn, Wikipedia. The identification of the man in the photograph is the family’s.

What He Brought Home

The Oceania Notes

The two pieces of paper Allen holds up to the camera belong to a real and very short-lived series. For its Pacific holdings the Japanese Government printed only four denominations — half a shilling, one shilling, ten shillings and one pound — and Allen’s pair are the first and the last of them.

The Australian War Memorial’s own examples describe the half-shilling as a purple and light brown lithograph with a beach and palms, and the one pound as green and light blue with coconut palms at either border; both carry the words The Japanese Government across the face, serial letters in red block stamps, and what the Memorial calls the standard Japanese watermark of a quatrefoil kiri flower. Numismatists catalogue the series as the Japanese government-issued Oceanian pound, introduced in 1942 and abolished soon after the Co-Prosperity Sphere was dissolved in August 1945. Four denominations make it the shortest set Japan issued anywhere: money for British and Australian territory, for Guam, and for Japan’s own South Seas Mandate.

Sources: Japanese invasion currency: 1/2 Shilling note — Australian War Memorial; Japanese invasion currency: one pound note — Australian War Memorial; Japanese government-issued Oceanian pound; Japanese government-issued Oceanian pound (users: Gilbert and Ellice Islands, Nauru, New Guinea, British Solomon Islands, South Seas Mandate; “shortest set ... issued”; abolition after August 1945).

“When They Got Into Australia”

Allen was repeating what the Australian Government itself had told the public. On 25 September 1942 Canberra announced that Japanese banknotes “intended for circulation in Australia in the event of a successful invasion” had come into Commonwealth hands, and listed the same four denominations.

Historians have since taken that claim apart. The Imperial Japanese Navy did float a landing in northern Australia in December 1941, and the Imperial Japanese Army rejected it outright: it put the requirement at 150,000 to 250,000 men against the Navy’s 45,000 to 60,000, and would not thin the Kwantung Army to find them. By late February 1942 Japan had settled instead on cutting Australia off from America. Peter Stanley, for many years the Australian War Memorial’s principal historian, puts it flatly: no historian of standing believes the Japanese had a plan to invade Australia, and there is “not a skerrick of evidence.” What the notes really were is worth knowing all the same: money in pounds and shillings, because the places Japan had taken — New Guinea, the Solomons, the Gilberts, Nauru — used Australian currency, and Japan intended to keep them.

Sources: Japanese government-issued Oceanian pound (notes issued for Australian mandated territories and British Oceania); Oceania — Japanese Invasion Money (quotes the Australian Government announcement of 25 September 1942); Proposed Japanese invasion of Australia during World War II; Japanese Occupation Money during WW2 — Peter Dunn, Australia @ War.

“Zillions of Them Around”

On the quantity, his memory is exactly right. The 1942 Australian announcement noted that large quantities of the notes had already been captured by Allied forces in recent operations, and by the time the Fifth Air Force was working its way up the New Guinea coast the things were everywhere in Allied hands — out of abandoned Japanese pay chests and supply dumps, and off the dead.

One documented case is almost contemporary with Allen’s own year at Hollandia: Clinton Palmer of the American 49th Fighter Group went back to a Hollandia beach on 24 May 1944 to recover his outfit’s equipment and came away with Japanese occupation money taken from bodies lying there. The notes were abolished outright once Japan surrendered, and their worthlessness is precisely what made them free souvenirs; they travelled home in footlockers by the thousand and are still common collector pieces today. His verdict — absolutely worthless — was a fair valuation then and is close to a fair one now.

Sources: Oceania — Japanese Invasion Money (Australian Government statement: large quantities captured in recent operations); Japanese Occupation Money during WW2 — Peter Dunn, Australia @ War (Clinton Palmer, Hollandia, 24 May 1944); Japanese government-issued Oceanian pound (withdrawal in 1945; collector status); Japanese Occupation Money during WW2 — Peter Dunn, Australia @ War (Clinton Palmer, 49th Fighter Group).

The Pass That Was Dropped

The second paper is a safe conduct pass, among the most heavily produced Allied leaflets of the Pacific war. Two organisations made them in Allen’s theatre.

The Far Eastern Liaison Office, an Australian-run propaganda and field intelligence unit set up on General Blamey’s orders on 19 June 1942, turned out leaflets by the tens of millions, in many languages and dialects, between November 1942 and its dissolution in September 1945; its output is set out below, with the two Pidgin sheets. Its surrender leaflets, its news sheets and its nostalgia leaflets went down over Papua, New Guinea and the Netherlands East Indies. From June 1944 the Psychological Warfare Branch of MacArthur’s own headquarters, under Brigadier General Bonner Fellers, did the same work on the American side. Both relied on aircraft to scatter the paper over Japanese positions, and the American air arm in the Southwest Pacific was the Fifth Air Force — the same numbered air force Allen’s 90th Squadron and 3rd Bomb Group belonged to. That is explanation enough for how a communications sergeant on the ground ended up with one in his pocket.

Sources: Far Eastern Liaison Office; Guide to the Far Eastern Liaison Office (FELO) Leaflet Collection — Australian War Memorial; Leaflets from the Southwest Pacific Area — USASOC History Office (ARSOF History); Bonner Frank Fellers and Psychological Warfare Leaflets in World War II — Hoover Institution.

Why “Surrender” Became “Cease Resistance”

Allen was reading verbatim, and the leaflet he was holding is in this collection. Its English side prints exactly what he says into the camera: “The bearer of this pass is voluntarily surrendering and should be treated with courtesy, and conducted to the nearest Commanding Officer for transfer to a rear area,” signed Commander-in-Chief, Allied Forces. For a while there was nothing to go on but his voice on the tape; the sheet itself has since been photographed, and the two agree word for word. The bluntness is the point, because that kind of wording was the very thing the Allies learned they had to change.

Japanese prisoners under interrogation explained that the words for surrender, kosan and kofuku, carried a disgrace no leaflet could talk a man past, and the Field Service Code issued to every soldier by War Minister Tojo on 8 January 1941 told him “never live to experience shame as a prisoner.” So the passes were rewritten — American psychological warfare staff and the Australian Far Eastern Liaison Office each claimed the idea — so that the bearer had ceased resistance rather than surrendered, which let a man come in without ever having said the word. The later text runs: the bearer has ceased resistance, treat him well in accordance with international law, take him to the nearest commanding officer. American records show the change tracking a real shift, from one prisoner per hundred Japanese dead in November 1944 to one in seven by July 1945.

Sources: The “I Cease Resistance” Safe Conduct Passes to the Japanese of WWII — PsyWarrior; Senjinkun military code (Field Service Code, 8 January 1941); Field service code (Senjinkun), adopted by the War Dept. 8 January 1941 — National Library of Australia catalogue; Far Eastern Liaison Office (FELO credited with proposing the “cease resistance” wording).

The office that printed them

The two Pidgin sheets Allen brought home belong to a paper war the Australians fought alongside the bombing one. The Far Eastern Liaison Office was set up on 19 June 1942 under the orders of the Allied Land Commander, General Sir Thomas Blamey, and became one of four sections of the Allied Intelligence Bureau established on 6 July 1942; it was commanded by Commander J. C. R. Proud of the Royal Australian Naval Volunteer Reserve, who set down its charge in his own words: all combat propaganda in the South-West Pacific, meaning the lowering of morale in the enemy forces, the misleading of the enemy about Allied military intentions, and the influencing of native peoples in Japanese-occupied areas.

Its chief instrument was the printed sheet. The Australian War Memorial, which holds the surviving collection in forty-nine series along with miscellaneous papers and six albums, records that from November 1942 until FELO’s dissolution in September 1945 the office estimated it had produced over 60 million leaflets in fourteen different languages or dialects. The American psychological-warfare literature is less tidy: 69 million in fourteen languages on one page, more than 58 million in six languages and numerous native dialects on another. The honest figure is tens of millions, and no single number will do. Pidgin was no footnote in that output: the Memorial’s finding aid catalogues four series addressed to the people of New Guinea in pidgin, coded P, PF, PK and PM, and Series P alone runs to at least ninety-five numbered leaflets, with further series for New Guinea villagers filed under other codes again. Allen’s sheets carry the Commonwealth coat of arms because Papua and the Mandated Territory of New Guinea were Australian-administered ground, though the arms alone cannot tell us which office set the type: the military administration on the islands, ANGAU, spoke to villagers in the government’s name too.

Sources: Guide to the Far Eastern Liaison Office (FELO) Leaflet Collection, Australian War Memorial; Far Eastern Liaison Office (Wikipedia); The United States PSYOP Organization in the Pacific During World War II (psywarrior.com); US Army Psychological Warfare Branch Leaflets for the Pacific, World War II (psywarrior.com).

“Most of it was Pidgin English”

Asked what language he met along the New Guinea coast, Allen answered plainly that most of it was Pidgin English, and that the people there had been in contact with missionaries before the war. The language he was hearing is called Tok Pisin. It grew out of the nineteenth-century Pacific labour trade, when Melanesians were recruited, and in some cases kidnapped, to work plantations in Queensland and later in Samoa; when labourers were returned to New Britain and New Ireland they carried the pidgin with them, where it stabilised and took in words from German and from indigenous languages, above all the Tolai tongue Kuanua of East New Britain.

By the early twentieth century it had become the working lingua franca between the administration and the villages — the language of missionaries, and of the government’s patrol officers, the kiaps. More than eight hundred separate languages were spoken in the territory, and no single indigenous one could carry an order across a district. That is precisely why the administration printed its leaflets in Tok Pisin rather than in English. The wartime label “Pidgin English” has not aged well: Tok Pisin is now an official language of Papua New Guinea with several million speakers, and linguists class it as a creole rather than a pidgin because so many people learn it first. Allen’s ear was accurate even if the name he used has since been retired.

Sources: APiCS Online, Survey chapter: Tok Pisin (Max Planck Institute); Tok Pisin (Wikipedia); Kiap (Wikipedia).

The warning leaflet: “LUKAUT” likely

The first sheet is a warning. What follows is a rendering of it rather than an official translation, and it runs roughly like this: watch out; we are bringing the fighting into your country now; take the women and the children out of the village and hide in the bush, lest the bombs strike them along with the Japanese; you men, wait for the word of the police and of our soldiers; this is the word of the Government. Two details in the Pidgin repay attention. The leaflet says mipala karim pait, using the exclusive form of “we,” the one that pointedly does not include the listener: the Allies are telling villagers that somebody else’s fight is about to arrive over their gardens. And the warning turns on kilim, a verb that in Tok Pisin need not mean killing outright — the unambiguous sense is carried by kilim i dai pinis.

The instruction to await the word of the polisboi and of “our soldiers” is a picture of how the administration actually reached a village: through the kiap, his appointed luluai and tultul, and the government’s indigenous constables, not through any direct approach by aircrew. The opening line, ol boi bilong kiap bilong bik Nukini, is the hardest to render with confidence; a closely comparable leaflet discussed by former patrol officers opens “Ol Luluai na Tultul na Lapun boi bilong Gavaman,” translated there as all luluais, tultuls and all government employees, which suggests Allen’s sheet likewise addresses the people in the government’s service across the New Guinea mainland, and “boi” here is the colonial usage of the day, now rightly understood as demeaning. No catalogued FELO series number has been found that fits this exact sheet. It is a FELO-type warning leaflet, then, and not, so far as anyone can show, a particular one.

Sources: English-Tok Pisin Dictionary (Humboldt University of Berlin teaching materials); Tok Pisin (Wikipedia), on the inclusive/exclusive first person; Wartime leaflet addressed to New Guinea Natives, Ex-Kiap Network Forum; Kiap (Wikipedia), on luluai and tultul.

The food leaflet: “selim nating” likely

The second sheet, printed in colour with an illustration, is an offer rather than a warning. Again the English is a rendering and not an official translation: our Government has plenty of food ready and is handing it out for nothing. The grammar is doing deliberate work. Where the warning leaflet says mipala, excluding the listener, this one says GUVMENT BELONG YUMI, using the inclusive “we” that takes in speaker and hearer together, so the government is presented as belonging to the villager as much as to the Australians; and selim nating, selling for nothing, is the plain Tok Pisin way of saying it costs you nothing.

The offer was not empty. The Australian New Guinea Administrative Unit, formed on 21 March 1942, was charged with the civil administration of Papua and the Mandated Territory, and its duties expressly included the rehabilitation of the local inhabitants in reoccupied areas: rebuilding villages and gardens, and feeding and doctoring people in districts the fighting had passed through. Where gardens had been eaten out, burned, or abandoned under occupation, a free ration was both relief and argument. As with the warning sheet, no catalogued series number fits this one, and nothing shows whether it was printed while the fighting was on or during the rehabilitation that followed. Both questions are open, and it is only honest to say so.

Sources: English-Tok Pisin Dictionary (Humboldt University of Berlin teaching materials); Tok Pisin (Wikipedia), on yumi as the inclusive first person plural; Australian New Guinea Administrative Unit (Wikipedia); ANGAU rehabilitation, Bougainville (Australian War Memorial film record C188850).

Brown paper parcels tied with string likely

Leaflets are only propaganda once they are on the ground, and getting them there was an aircrew problem. The American psychological-warfare literature records that FELO’s bundles were at first dropped in brown paper parcels tied with string, and that the office later adopted what was called the Weigall Device, a time fuse that opened the bundle at a preset altitude so the sheets scattered over a village or a camp instead of thumping into the trees in a block. Commander Proud recorded that a team of FELO officers and non-commissioned officers was attached to RAAF and American air force squadrons, flying on leaflet-dropping missions and briefed to pinpoint the targets.

In the Pacific the work was done by whatever was going, C-47 transports, B-24s, fighters and B-25 Mitchells among them, and fighter pilots are said to have disliked the job because it meant flying low and slow within reach of anti-aircraft fire. That puts leaflets like Allen’s squarely in the traffic of the war he served in. No leaflet sortie by the 90th Bomb Squadron has turned up, though, and nothing says how these two sheets came into his hands. He was candid about the same gap elsewhere in the interview, saying of the Japanese invasion money that he knew there were zillions of them around but did not know how he got hold of them.

Sources: The United States PSYOP Organization in the Pacific During World War II (psywarrior.com), on brown paper parcels and the Weigall Device; The Navy’s Secret War in the Pacific, Naval Historical Society of Australia, Commander Proud on FELO teams attached to RAAF and USAAF squadrons; US Army Psychological Warfare Branch Leaflets for the Pacific, World War II (psywarrior.com), on aircraft used for leaflet drops.

Memorial Day, Cressona, 2006

The Drum in These Pictures Belongs to His Own Post

The bass drum lettered CRESSONA BAND is the drum of an organisation the American Legion put back together. The band was chartered on 31 March 1902 as the Cressona Cornet Band, was absorbed into the Bressler Band of Schuylkill Haven from 1911 to 1922, and in April 1922 was reorganised as the Cressona Band under American Legion Post 286 — the post of which Allen was a sixty-year member and a past commander.

Instruments were fetched out of attics and cellars and stored in a member’s living room until players could be found for them. In August 1956, four months after the Cressona News page reproduced in the previous chapter, the band voted to build its own hall; a plot on Wilson Street was given, and the first rehearsal in the new building was held in February 1957. The band rehearses there still.

Sources: The Cressona Band, Band History, compiled by John P. Shoener.

Where These Photographs Were Made

Not at the cemetery. There are no headstones and no burial rows in any frame. One shows a rough granite boulder set with a bronze tablet, a flagpole, a clipped hedge with small flags planted along it, overhead wires and a red commercial sign at the roadside; the wide frame of the service shows banner-draped tables and a lectern on open grass, a chain-link fence and a stack of pallets behind, and timber steps climbing the bank.

That is the American Legion Plaza on Front Street, where the parade ends and the programme is held. The borough cemetery is on Ash Street, half a mile off. The distinction matters, because Allen spent twenty years running the other place, and because the observance now comes in two parts: a Legion service at the cemetery about half past seven in the morning, and the parade afterwards, forming at the aluminium works at nine and ending at the plaza.

Sources: Republican & Herald, “Around the region,” 15 May 2017 (parade route); Reading Eagle, Cressona Legion Memorial Day notice, 14 April 2024; Cressona Borough Council minutes, 3 February 2020.

He Described a Memorial Day That Had Already Stopped

These photographs were made in the summer of 2006. Eight months earlier, a reporter asked him what he remembered best about the cemetery work, and he answered with something that had already gone: that years ago, at the end of each Memorial Day parade, the children in the parade would place flowers or flags on the graves of veterans. “That was a big day. It was a meeting place for people coming back to visit their old friends.”

The same article says plainly that the parade no longer goes to the cemetery. What he described, then, was already gone, and these pictures show what was left of the day: the band in the street, the Legion service, and small flags ranked along a hedge at a memorial stone rather than set on graves. Nobody wrote down the year the parade stopped going, and nothing since has turned it up.

Sources: Andrea T. Turano, “The borough cemetery,” Republican & Herald, 16 October 2005, p. C1.

A Card That Is Not From the War

Kept with the wartime papers, but twenty years younger than they are.

The trading card is not from the war

The card in the collection can be pinned down exactly, and doing so takes it out of the war altogether. Its reverse carries the words BATTLE CARDS beside the number 34, the copyright mark of T.C.G. — initials Topps used for its chewing gum business — and the line PRINTED IN U.S.A. The front carries its title in a yellow panel set inside a white frame, exactly as collectors describe cards 1 to 53 of the set. Those are the marks of card 34, Saving His Buddies, from Topps’ sixty-six-card set Battle, issued in 1965. A near-identical English set was put out by A. & B. C. of London, running to seventy-three cards — the seven extra ones leaders and fighters, with a checklist at the end — and later British printings quietly dropped four of the bloodiest scenes.

But the card here names its own country of manufacture along the foot, so it is the American original and not the British issue. So it is not something Allen carried home in a barracks bag in 1944. It reached the family twenty years after his discharge at Fort Dix on 8 June 1945, and nobody wrote down whose hand put it in the box.

Sources: 1965 Topps Battle: The Story of World War II — Trading Card Database; 1965 Topps Battle Trading Cards Checklist, Set Info, Buying Guide — Cardboard Connection; Non-Sports Cards — 1965 Topps Battle, PSA CardFacts; 1965 A&BC Battle Cards (England) — Dean’s Cards.

Pvt. Donaldson was never a man

The printed story on the reverse follows a scout named Pvt. Donaldson who comes on two wounded Americans propped against a tree, feels a rifle shoved into his back, is ordered by a Japanese soldier to help carry the two men — ‘these two dogs’ — to an enemy camp, and is delivered when a marine leaps out of a tree and puts a knife into their captor. No action of the kind turns up in any record, and no soldier of that name has ever been traced. The Battle set was scripted by Len Brown of Topps with Woody Gelman and painted by Norman Saunders, and it invented its soldiers freely; collectors have catalogued its Capt. Dis Hassin bailing out of a burning plane and its Brad Barnett taken by the Germans, and Brown himself put the share of his made-up captions at eighty to eighty-five percent.

The last thirteen cards then set all of it alongside genuine men — de Gaulle, Churchill, MacArthur. The card belongs in the case, but it has to be labelled as 1965 commercial fiction, so that no reader takes Pvt. Donaldson for a man in the 90th Squadron or the scene for anything Allen witnessed.

Sources: More Topps Trading Card Madness — The Bedlam Files; 1965 Topps Battle Trading Cards Checklist, Set Info, Buying Guide — Cardboard Connection; Collecting the 1965 Topps Battle Set — PSA; Battle! Story in Text (Bob Heffner) — independent transcription of card 34, ‘Saving his Buddies’.

Battle, 1965: the last of the serious war cards likely

Battle ran to sixty-six cards of two and a half by three and a half inches, the first fifty-three of them combat scenes and the last thirteen given over to commanders, statesmen and checklists. Norman Saunders, who had painted Mars Attacks for the same company three years before, supplied the pictures, and every pack also held a U.S. Military Cloth Emblem, two inches by three and five-sixteenths, one of twenty-four unit patches — 7th Army, 2nd Division, the 442nd Regimental Combat Team — with card 65 printed as their checklist.

Collectors read the set as a mash-up of the Philadelphia war cards of 1938 and Topps’ own Civil War News of 1962, and the link is a real one: Len Brown and Woody Gelman had built Civil War News first. It did not sell well, and collectors’ accounts agree that its failure ended the company’s straight-faced war sets, after which its non-sport output turned to humour. The only chewing gum anywhere in Allen’s own account is the stick he reached into his bag for on Christmas Eve in the jungle, when he brought his hand out with a scorpion hanging on his fingers and had to go to the medics before the picture started.

Sources: 1965 Topps Battle Trading Cards Checklist, Set Info, Buying Guide — Cardboard Connection; Non-Sports Cards — 1965 Topps Battle, PSA CardFacts; More Topps Trading Card Madness — The Bedlam Files; Collecting the 1965 Topps Battle Set — PSA.

The wartime cards this one imitates, and the age he was when they sold

The format — a painted horror on the front, a caption in a panel, a story and a number on the back — was worked out in Philadelphia before the war by Gum, Inc. Horrors of War came out in 1938 and reached two hundred and eighty-eight cards in two series, the first two hundred and forty on the wars in Spain, Ethiopia and China and the rest on the Japanese border clashes and Hitler’s move into the Sudetenland; every back closed with the line that to know the horrors of war is to want peace, and Roosevelt is said to have used them to show an isolationist country what was happening. War News Pictures, a hundred and forty-four cards, followed in 1939 and 1940 and was advertised openly as the sequel; War Gum, a hundred and thirty-two cards, ran from 1941 into 1942 and turned from enemy atrocity toward Allied heroes and commanders.

They were sold to children with bubble gum, and they caused a stir in the press from the day they appeared. What is worth noticing here is his age. Born on 9 September 1918, Allen was nineteen when the first of them reached the candy counter and twenty-three when the last did, already in Battery F and then in uniform for the duration; he was never the boy buying the war for pocket money, he was the young man the pictures were being made about.

Sources: 1938 Gum Inc. Horrors of War Trading Cards — Cardboard Connection; Non-Sports Cards — 1938 Horrors Of War, PSA CardFacts; War Gum (132), Gum, Inc. (R164) United States — Skytamer; War News Pictures (144), Gum, Inc. (R165) United States — Skytamer.

The card sells the hatred; he only admits it

Set the card beside what Allen actually said about the enemy and the contrast is the reason to keep it. His only sight of prisoners came at Hollandia, where the infantry had gone in a day or two ahead of him and he stood on the beach with no orders while a column of captured men was marched past. Reading out a Japanese-language surrender pass that promised the bearer courtesy and conduct to the rear, he answered it himself, saying it all depended on the mood of the captor, that after what they pulled in the Philippines there was not too much love for the Japanese, and then breaking off in the middle of the old slogan about the only good Japanese rather than finish it.

Somewhere in that same exchange — the tape carries no speaker marks, so it may be the interviewer closing the thought — comes the reason a captor might not honour the promise, and it arrives in the very word the card uses for its title: what the enemy had maybe just done to one of the guy’s buddies. The card turns that feeling into an adventure with a knife in it and a happy ending. He states it as a fact about himself and his war, hesitates, and leaves the sentence unfinished.
