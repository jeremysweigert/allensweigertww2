V-Mail

The censorship Allen describes — envelopes left unsealed for an officer to read before sending — was standard practice; V-Mail (Victory Mail) was the answer to what came next. A letter written on special stationery was censored, then photographed onto microfilm, shipped home at a fraction of the bulk of the original paper, and printed back out at about one-quarter size for delivery. A letter on microfilm took roughly 1/37th the cargo space of the same letter on paper; over a billion V-Mails were sent between June 1942 and November 1945.

Source: National Postal Museum; The National WWII Museum.

In Memoriam

The 3rd Bomb Group's own history book closes its record of the war with a memorial roll: roughly 350 names of the group's own men who did not come home, prefaced by a short poem signed Edward Fenton — “All that I have I give. Yet all the world's abounding words cannot decrease my wonderment unending, as death, my sole release, buys your salvation: peace.” Allen's own name is not on that list; he is one of the ones who did come home, which is the reason this document exists to tell his story at all.

Source: The Reaper's Harvest, 3rd Attack Group Association.

Documents He Brought Home

The New Guinea field newspaper, a signed occupation note, and Japanese-language leaflets Allen kept from his service.

Psychological Warfare Branch Leaflets

General MacArthur's Southwest Pacific Area Psychological Warfare Branch, formed in Brisbane in June 1944, produced close to 200 different leaflets in its “J1” numbered series — small codes like “25J1” or “159J1” printed directly on the leaflet identified which one it was. The Sixth Army's own Psywar Branch, formed that same September at Hollandia, used a separate “J6” series. No printed code is visible in these particular photographs, so which specific leaflet numbers Allen's are couldn't be confirmed without a clearer scan of the corners and margins where the SWPA numbering was usually stamped.

Sources: psywarrior.com; arsof-history.org.
