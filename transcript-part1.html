The tea that stopped the ship likely

What Allen watched on the Brisbane wharf was the smoko, and it was not idleness. The smoko is a sanctioned pause in Australian working life, taken mid-morning and again in the afternoon, named from the call to down tools for a smoke — the earliest spellings end in an H, “smoke-oh” — traceable to the Victorian goldfields of the 1850s and first recorded in print in a Queensland paper, the Moreton Bay Courier. It began as a tobacco break, but by 1942 it was as fixed a part of the working day as the day itself and the billy had long since joined the pipe.

To newly landed Americans, who had just spent twenty-five days at sea and wanted their kit off the ship, a wharf that simply stopped was incomprehensible. Friction over Australian waterfront practice was real and there were ugly incidents in Queensland ports that year, but how far it amounted to obstruction of the war effort is still argued over by historians and should not be settled in a caption. Allen’s own telling carries no bitterness in it at all, only astonishment, and that is probably the truest note to keep.

Pounds, shillings and florins

Australia in 1942 still ran on British money, and nothing about it mapped onto a dollar. A pound was twenty shillings and a shilling was twelve pence, which made two hundred and forty pence to the pound, and the coins in Allen’s hand would have run from the halfpenny and penny up through threepence, sixpence and the shilling to the florin, which was two shillings. There was no Australian half-crown at all — almost alone among the sterling countries, Australia never issued one — and the five-shilling crown had been struck only in 1937 and 1938 and was already unpopular.

Notes came in ten shillings, one pound, five pounds and ten. On top of the arithmetic there was an exchange problem: from the devaluation of December 1931 until decimal currency arrived in 1966 the Australian pound was held at sixteen shillings sterling, so it was not even worth the same as the British pound whose names it borrowed. A young man from Schuylkill County handing a five-dollar bill across a PX counter and getting back a fistful of unfamiliar silver had every reason not to know what he had.

A different gauge in every state

Allen had this right as a fact about Australia and slightly wrong about his own journey, and the difference is worth keeping. The Australian colonies had built their railways to different gauges before federation, and on the east coast the break bit hardest at Wallangarra on the Queensland-New South Wales border, where Queensland’s 3 ft 6 in narrow gauge met New South Wales’s 4 ft 8 1/2 in standard gauge across a single island platform, each side under its own railway’s signage, and every man and every crate had to change trains.

The war made the problem notorious: fourteen breaks of gauge were still in use in 1939, upwards of 1,600 service personnel and many more civilians were employed shifting 1.8 million tons of freight across them, and rail movement in Australia was not brought under central direction until early 1943. Allen’s own run, however, was entirely inside Queensland, Brisbane to Townsville on the North Coast line and then west on the Great Northern to Charters Towers, both of them 3 ft 6 in. The change he remembers at Townsville was a change of line and of train, not of gauge, though the story he was told aboard, that every state used a different track, was perfectly true.

A beacon the enemy could use too likely

The property that brought Allen’s aircraft home was the same one that worried the Navy. A broadcast station is a free beacon for anyone with a loop aerial, and Australian authorities acted on it: 4KA at Ayr, on the coast some fifty miles south-east of Townsville, was banned by the Navy from broadcasting at night from 3 March 1942 to 12 April 1943, on the grounds that its signal could be used as a homing device by enemy bombers. Allen’s flight was a daylight one — he describes a clear run over the Coral Sea before the storm forced them down to the waves — so a north Queensland station would have been on the air for him. Whether 4TO itself came under the same night restriction is not recorded in the sources consulted here. The detail is worth keeping anyway, because it shows how thin the line was between navigation aid and liability in 1942.

Homing on Townsville likely

The radio compass on that flight deck was almost certainly the SCR-269, standardised in January 1940: a BC-433 receiver, a rotatable loop aerial, a BC-434 control box and an I-81 or I-82 needle indicator, tuning 200 to 1,750 kilocycles, a range that swallows the whole commercial broadcast band. Tune it to a strong station and the needle points at the transmitter, which is precisely the procedure Allen describes to the co-pilot. The station is harder to pin down than the method.

Townsville’s commercial station was 4TO, opened by AWA on 5 October 1931 — the mayor at the microphone, two hundred local artists in the Theatre Royal — running only about 200 watts through the war years and not raised to 500 until 1948; a Townsville broadcast year book for 1946-47 records it at 384 metres, 780 kilocycles. The ABC’s 4QN, opened 26 November 1936, also served the town. No Townsville broadcaster has been found on 610 kilocycles, and Allen himself hedged the figure sixty-odd years after the fact; the number is uncertain, the technique is not.

“Revent” Was a Revetment

One word in the transcript looks like machine-transcription noise and is in fact a precise piece of airfield vocabulary. Allen describes “a different place, cut out, called Revent, where you parked the planes.” He is saying revetment — an earth-walled bay, bulldozed or dug out well away from the runway, into which a single aircraft was taxied so that a bomb falling on one machine would not take the whole squadron with it.

At 7-Mile the Americans began building them in earnest right after the heavy Japanese raid of 17 August 1942, and a few of those bomber revetments survived at Jacksons airport into this century. Sixty-odd years on, Allen was still reaching for the right technical term for a hole in a Papuan hillside.

The crocodile above the pool

Three crocodiles are now recognised on New Guinea. The saltwater or estuarine crocodile, Crocodylus porosus, is the largest living reptile, with the largest reliably measured animal at a total length of at least 615 centimetres, and it ranges from southwestern India and Sri Lanka through southeast Asia and the Indonesian archipelago to the Solomons; it tolerates everything from fresh water to full-strength sea water and moves well up rivers and into swamps and lagoons.

The New Guinea crocodile, Crocodylus novaeguineae, is a smaller freshwater animal, the males reaching about three and a half metres, living in the swamps, marshes and lakes of the northern half of the island; a third, Hall’s New Guinea crocodile, was separated from it in 2019 and belongs to the country south of the central ranges. Allen’s squadron was on the northern side, which leaves the saltwater crocodile and the New Guinea crocodile as the candidates, and the record does not settle which one it was. The explanation he was given, that the carcass would be left to lie until the evil spirits were out of it before it was butchered, came from an Australian standing beside him rather than from the men who had killed it, and it is best read as one European’s gloss on a practice he had watched, not as any Papuan’s account of his own belief.

Why the villages were so quiet

Allen noticed that the people kept their distance and that he saw few of them, and there is a documented reason beyond shyness. From 21 March 1942 the Australian New Guinea Administrative Unit, ANGAU, governed civil affairs in both Papua and the Mandated Territory, and its largest single task was raising and directing indigenous labour for the Australian and American forces. At its September 1944 peak more than thirty-five thousand men were involved in the organisation, directed by some two thousand Australian officers, first carrying and portering, later building; the airstrips, roads and supply dumps the Americans used were in substantial part their work.

Recruitment was not voluntary: an order of June 1942 let ANGAU contract Papuans for up to three years and forbade them to desert or refuse work, and the carrier rolls went from about two thousand that month to over sixteen thousand by December. The cost is on the record: at least 2,024 ANGAU carriers and labourers died during the war, mainly of infectious disease such as malaria and dysentery, with 46 killed by enemy action and 91 wounded. A village whose fit men were away under indenture would look to a passing airman much as Allen describes it. This is a group-level fact about the Territory rather than a finding about the particular hamlets he walked into, which the record does not identify.

The bag, and the man with the stick likely

What Allen saw tied over the woman’s forehead was a bilum, the looped string bag of New Guinea, made by hand from spun plant fibre in a technique passed down between generations. Its band goes across the forehead so that the bag rides on the back and the carrier’s hands stay free, and the bag stretches to hold whatever is put into it; the Museum of New Zealand’s catalogue note on one collected example says bilums are used by men and women to carry yams, taro and other foodstuffs, and sometimes even babies. What the briefings told them: 32 to 34 years, and do not go west likely

Two things Allen carried away from his briefings are worth unpicking. A life expectancy of 32 to 34 years is a period life-table figure, an average across everyone born, and in a population with heavy infant and child mortality it is dragged down hard by deaths in the first few years; it never meant that the adults in front of him would be dead at 33, and populations with such figures routinely contain old people. No census had counted most of the interior in 1943, so any number handed to American troops was an estimate rather than a measurement.

The rule against going west rested less on a proven belt of headhunters than on the limits of the colonial administration itself: the country beyond the patrol posts was, in the administration’s own language, uncontrolled, and the last of the great exploring patrols into it, led by the patrol officer Jim Taylor with John Black, came out of the country between Mount Hagen and the Sepik in 1939, only a few years before Allen reached New Guinea — a journey the historian Bill Gammage reconstructed in The Sky Travellers. Headhunting is documented for particular peoples in particular places, but the blanket claim he was given was a colonial commonplace rather than a description of the country a day’s walk inland from his strip. The order also made plain military sense, because west and north-west was where the Japanese were. His words here come through the transcript garbled, and are given as recorded.

The missionaries, and what had happened at Dobodura likely

When Allen said the people along that coast had been in contact with the missionaries, he was describing a ruin rather than an institution. The Anglican mission of the Territory of Papua held stations on the north coast at Gona and inland at Sangara and Isivita, in Orokaiva country, and the Japanese came ashore at Gona on 21 July 1942. In the months that followed the Gona party of a priest, a nursing sister and a teacher was broken up, and the two women were killed on Buna beach; the Sangara and Isivita party of two priests, a layman, a nurse and a teacher was taken by villagers and handed over to the Japanese at Dobodura, whence they were taken to Buna and killed.

Dobodura is the plain the Americans then turned into a complex of fifteen landing grounds, and where Allen’s squadron moved on 21 May 1943, nine months after that handover. Churches commemorate 333 church workers of all denominations, Papuan and foreign, killed in New Guinea in 1942 and 1943, on 2 September. That villagers did the handing over belongs in the account as well, and it cannot be read simply as treachery, because the Orokaiva were caught between two armies in their own gardens and had reasons of their own which the mission’s wartime publications were in no position to weigh.

Which pidgin he was hearing

Allen remembered the contact language on the coast as Pidgin English, and in one sense he was right, in that the Americans were meeting a trade language rather than a village one. But the language that carried across the Territory of Papua, where he spent 1943 at Port Moresby and Dobodura, was not Tok Pisin. It was Police Motu, a simplified form of the Motu language of the Port Moresby coast, which spread with the Papuan constabulary — hence the name — and became the administration’s working tongue through Papua, and which was officially renamed Hiri Motu in the early 1970s, on the reasoning that the language’s history was older than the name Police Motu implied.

Tok Pisin, the English-vocabulary pidgin that Americans usually meant by the phrase, had grown out of the Pacific labour trade and the plantations of the Bismarck Archipelago, and spread through the former German territory in the north; its expansion south into Papua was, in one survey’s words, much more restricted until recent years. Allen would have come within its range only in February 1944, at Nadzab in the Markham valley. The likeliest reading is that Pidgin English was his catch-all for whatever contact speech he heard, which is what the phrase generally meant in an American’s mouth in 1943.

The switchboard with the little flappers

The board Allen calls an antique was almost certainly one of the Signal Corps’ local-battery field switchboards, the six-line BD-71 or its twelve-line twin, the BD-72. Each line carried a hinged shutter, a “drop”, released by the ringing current when a subscriber wound the hand crank on his telephone, so the shutter fell open and showed the operator which line was calling; a night alarm buzzed until it was reset. The operator listened on a chest set, plugged a cord into the called party’s jack and turned the board’s own magneto crank, about a hundred volts, to ring the bell at the far end.

The instruments on the other ends of those lines were EE-8 field telephones, adopted in 1935, powered by dry cells with a hand-cranked generator for signalling and good for something like seven miles of field wire. Allen was right about the design if not the date: cord-and-drop switchboards were a nineteenth-century idea still standard issue in 1942, because they needed nothing but batteries, wire and a man’s arm. The wire was the other half of the job, and the hard half — he remembers baked Australian ground that had to be dug or the line swung up through trees to cross a dirt road, and lines kept off the mud in New Guinea.

Where The Warning Came From

The alert that emptied Allen’s part of the strip came out of one of the more remarkable improvisations of the Papuan campaign. In January 1942 Major Donald Small, chief signals officer of the 8th Military District, threw a ring of wireless observation posts around Port Moresby at a radius of roughly 150 kilometres; the New Guinea Air Warning Wireless, known to everyone as the Spotters, grew from sixteen stations to sixty-one with 180 men by the end of 1942, and to more than 150 posts by late 1944, absorbing Army, RAAF and civilian coastwatcher stations under one command from October 1942. Two rings did the work.

The outer gave about an hour’s notice; the inner ring, at some fifty kilometres, gave ten minutes. Radar helped too, and on 12 April 1943 the incoming formation was tracked by the set atop Paga Hill as it crossed the range. That arithmetic is exactly the experience Allen describes: against high-flying aircraft coming over the mountains the warning arrived measured in minutes, and a formation at 25,000 feet could be directly overhead before anyone on the field had looked up.

The Tent With the Unsealed Envelope

The procedure Allen describes was the Army’s standard method, and the unsealed envelope was the whole point of it. A soldier overseas was forbidden to use a civilian mailbox; War Department Pamphlet 21-1, issued 29 July 1943, told him under the heading “Enlisted Men” to “Place your letter unsealed in your organization mail box, never in any civil post office box,” and reminded him, “You are required to use the Army Postal Service, and the Army Postal Service only.” A censor officer of his own organization read it before it travelled on, and the Army required mail to be “completely through censorship within 48 hours.” The signature he may have seen belonged to the officers’ rule alongside his own: an officer censored his own letter, sealed it, and signed the lower left-hand corner, his mail still “subject to further censorship examination by base censorship detachments.” Ten “don’ts” were set before him.

Eight were subjects — the location, strength, materiel and equipment of Army units, military installations, transportation facilities, convoys with their routes, ports and time en route, movements of ships, troops and aircraft, plans and forecasts for future operations, the effects of enemy operations, and casualties before official release — and two were habits: no code or cipher, and no disclosing his location except as authorized. Why Nobody Turned On A Light likely

Allen supplies his own explanation, and it is the one the record supports. A single aircraft circling in the dark did little harm by itself; its real work was to scout, to keep men out of their cots, and, when it carried flares, to mark for someone else. On the night of 13-14 October 1942 at Henderson Field the battleships Kongo and Haruna opened fire at 01:33 and shelled the Lunga perimeter for 83 minutes with more than 900 fourteen-inch rounds, destroying 48 of the 90 aircraft on the field, burning nearly all the aviation fuel and killing forty-one men.

Set against a risk like that, a searchlight beam and a stream of tracer amounted to a free fix on the runway and on the gun pits themselves. The contrast with daylight is stark: during the 12 April 1943 raid the Australian anti-aircraft batteries around Port Moresby fired 1,112 rounds and claimed no hits at all. By day there was nothing left to conceal; by night there was everything.

The Joker Who Circled: Washing Machine Charlie

Allen never gives the aeroplane a name; he calls it the joker. Allied troops across the Pacific had a name for it, and it came from the sound. A pilot flying a twin-engined machine could throw his engines out of synchronisation to produce a slow, uneven throb, loud enough to wake a camp and then keep it awake, and so the lone nightly visitor became Washing Machine Charlie; the comparison to a pre-war Maytag is often repeated but we could not source it.

The types varied, from ship- and shore-based single-engined seaplanes of the R-Area Air Force to Mitsubishi G4M Betty bombers flown deliberately out of step, and the jobs varied with them: scouting, dropping flares for Japanese naval or ground forces, bombing airfields and installations, and simply keeping a whole base awake and braced. A companion nickname, Bedcheck Charlie, belonged chiefly to the lone Luftwaffe visitors over Europe but reached the Pacific too, in an Associated Press despatch from the Gilberts dated 12 January 1944. The names are best documented at Henderson Field and across the Solomons and travelled to other Allied bases; nothing in Allen’s account shows the 90th Bomb Squadron used them, so the nickname belongs on the page as context rather than as his word.

V-Mail

The censorship Allen describes — envelopes left unsealed for an officer to read before sending — was standard practice; V-Mail (Victory Mail) was the answer to what came next. A letter written on special stationery was censored, then photographed onto microfilm, shipped home at a fraction of the bulk of the original paper, and printed back out at about one-quarter size for delivery. A letter on microfilm took roughly 1/37th the cargo space of the same letter on paper; over a billion V-Mails were sent between June 1942 and November 1945.

APO 1106 — The Number That Isn’t There

Seven of Allen’s eight numbers decode. The first one does not, and the reason is worth recording precisely rather than guessing around. The Army Postal Service’s own master register of every APO used between January 1942 and November 1947 runs 1102, 1103, 1105, then jumps to 1112: there is no APO 1106 in it at all, and the surrounding 1100-series numbers were routed through New York and opened in 1945 and 1946 for occupied Europe, not the Pacific. The San Francisco-routed numbers above 1000 were assigned to the Philippines and the Ryukyus in 1945, after Allen was already home. So 1106 cannot be a place he served under, and the honest conclusion is that this figure is either something other than an APO — a unit number, a shipment or class number, a date — or a slip of the pen or of the copying. The news before the movie

The two voices Allen names were the two that carried to the Southwest Pacific. KGEI was built by General Electric for the Golden Gate International Exposition on Treasure Island in 1939, and moved in 1941 to the Redwood Peninsula three miles east of Belmont, California — a 50-kilowatt transmitter behind three-foot concrete walls, floors and ceilings. Christmas in the Southwest Pacific, and Somewhere to Go

The services worked hard to mark the day at the far end of the supply line. The Army Postal Service told families to mail Christmas parcels for men overseas between 15 September and 15 October, months ahead, and the mail itself came in two forms: ordinary letters, which the men much preferred — Navy personnel in 1944 got about six of them for every V-mail — and V-mail, the photographed and shrunken letter written on a single sheet that folded into its own envelope, of which more than a billion were processed between June 1942 and November 1945. Either way it came in surges, which is why Allen remembers weeks of nothing and then a whole bundle at once.

Turkey dinners were pushed forward wherever the logistics reached, and across the theater men improvised the day out of what was at hand. And there were the pictures. The Army’s motion picture service ran 260 film libraries feeding sixty bases on six continents, moving reels by boat, plane, train, automobile, motorcycle, camel caravan and Arctic dog sled, and in the South Pacific the men built their own theaters — benches cut from palm trunks and a white sheet hung from the branches. That is the theater Allen was headed to with a scorpion on his hand, and the one where, by his own account, he used to plug in his shortwave set and read the day’s news aloud before the show started, until another squadron ran a line up to his tent to hear it too.

The Scorpion in the Barracks Bag

Of roughly 2,770 described scorpion species, only about 104 — 3.8 percent, and almost all of them in the family Buthidae — are considered medically significant, and the dangerous ones are concentrated far from New Guinea: Androctonus, Buthus and Leiurus across North Africa, the Middle East and Asia, Tityus in South America and the Caribbean, Centruroides in Mexico, Central America and the American southwest, with Mexico, Brazil, Iran, Algeria and Morocco carrying the deaths. No Australasian region appears on that list. New Guinea’s own scorpions are a smaller cast — four genera in two families, the hormurids Hormurus and Liocheles alongside the buthids Isometrus and Lychas.

The Australian Museum, describing the twenty-nine species in six genera found across Australia, puts the likely outcome plainly: they can give a painful sting causing inflammation and pain for several hours, treated with a cold pack and a doctor if the pain persists. What the medics at Dobodura had to offer was therefore symptomatic — and for any puncture of the skin, a needle. The Army had been immunizing every soldier with tetanus toxoid since 1941, boosting with toxoid rather than horse serum at the time of a wound, a regime so effective that the whole Army recorded just twelve cases of tetanus in the war, one per million soldiers, and most of those twelve had not been fully immunized. Allen’s shot was most plausibly that, or morphine for the pain; the record does not settle which, and the honest reading of the episode is that he was in real pain, in no real danger, and back in his seat before the picture finished.

There Was No Station Yet: How the Music Got In likely

The Song He Still Cannot Hear Straight Through

Irving Berlin wrote “White Christmas” before the war — by one account in January 1940 — and Bing Crosby gave it its first public airing on his NBC program The Kraft Music Hall in the last days of December 1941, within weeks of Pearl Harbor; sources disagree on whether that broadcast fell on Christmas Eve or Christmas Day, and the point is not settled. Crosby recorded the version America knew on 29 May 1942 at Radio Recorders in Los Angeles, for Decca, with the John Scott Trotter Orchestra and the Ken Darby Singers, in a session that took eighteen minutes. It was released on 30 July 1942 in the six-disc album of 78s from the film Holiday Inn, won Best Original Song at the Academy Awards ceremony held in March 1943, and reached the top of the Billboard chart on 31 October 1942, where it stayed eleven weeks and on into the new year.

Crosby grew wary of singing it for men overseas: he said he hesitated because it invariably caused such a nostalgic yearning among the men that it made them sad — and by his own account they kept asking for it anyway. The pressing Allen would have heard in a tent in New Guinea was that original 1942 master, which Decca wore out from sheer use; Crosby had to re-cut the song on 19 March 1947 to replace it. Seventy years later he told his interviewer he had not heard it once that day without his thoughts going back, which is precisely the effect the singer had been afraid of.

The Prisoners on the Beach

What Allen saw was genuinely unusual. Hollandia was held by about 11,000 Japanese, but only some 500 of them were ground combat troops, drawn from antiaircraft batteries; the rest were service and headquarters personnel of the 6th Air Division of the Fourth Air Army and of the Second Army, and when the landings came at dawn on 22 April they broke and went into the bush, making for Genjem and the long overland route west to Sarmi.

Most Japanese losses at Hollandia therefore came during the weeks of mopping up that followed, with men taken in small groups — starving, sick and cut off. The US Army’s official history records that by 6 June 1944, “611 Japanese were captured and over 3,300 killed.” Its casualty table, whose New Guinea figures run to early October 1944, credits the Hollandia operation with 655 prisoners against 4,475 killed; set beside Aitape’s 100 prisoners taken against 8,825 killed, or Wakde-Sarmi’s 55, that is the largest take of prisoners anywhere in the campaign, with Biak’s 460 the next nearest. Mid-May, when Allen waded ashore, was precisely the season of those round-ups, and a column of prisoners moving along the beach is exactly what the record would predict.

Up the Coast by LST

Allen’s account of the move to Hollandia can be dated almost to the day from the official Army Air Forces history, which records that the 3rd Bombardment Group’s waterborne echelon, moving directly from Nadzab via Lae without staging, reached Humboldt Bay between 12 and 15 May 1944. That is his “got on boats up the coast of New Guinea”: down the Markham Valley to Lae, then north-west along the coast and into Humboldt Bay, and off the bow ramps of LSTs into the shallows, which is how ground echelons and their equipment came ashore there. The standard USAAF station list gives the group at Hollandia from 12 May 1944.

One point should be stated plainly rather than smoothed over: the infantry assault had gone in on 22 April, three weeks earlier, not “a day or two before” — sixty years on, the beach was still raw, and the memory has compressed the interval. The aircraft were not waiting for them either. The doldrums had given way to summer drought, and the captured strips were so dusty that on 16 May only one A-20 squadron could be called forward: the 8th, one of the four squadrons of Allen’s own group.

Why the May quarter was still there in September

On 5 April 1944 Fifth Air Force announced that ground personnel who had served “faithfully and continuously overseas for at least eighteen months” might be selected for return to the United States, with preference going to men with six months or more in the islands north of Australia. Allen qualified on both counts and more, but the quotas were minute — about one per cent of the air force’s total ground strength each month.

The entire June-to-August allotment for the 54th Troop Carrier Wing was one officer and five enlisted men. The Army’s own historians calculated that at 1944 rates a complete turnover of Far East Air Forces ground personnel would have taken more than eight years, and the Air Evaluation Board concluded flatly that “the rotation program as executed in the SWPA had a more adverse effect on morale than any other factor.” His one dry sentence about the May quarter still sitting there in September is that policy felt from the inside: his name was on a list, and the list was not moving — which is why the shouting in the camp on his birthday sounded to him at first like a cruel joke.

One ship, no escort likely

Allen’s ship cannot be identified from what he says, and it would be wrong to put a name to it. What he describes, though, is a recognisable type: pre-war passenger liners requisitioned in 1941-42 and run as fast troopships under the War Shipping Administration. Matson Line’s Lurline, Matsonia, Mariposa and Monterey had been built for the Hawaii and Australasia runs out of California, calling at Samoa, Fiji, New Zealand and Australia; all four passed into government hands within weeks of Pearl Harbor — Monterey was handed over at San Francisco on 3 December 1941, four days before the attack — and by Matson’s own reckoning the four made 119 wartime voyages and carried 736,000 troops, a count that comes from the company rather than from a government record.

Because they were fast they frequently ran without a convoy — Monterey “served as a fast troop carrier, often operating alone so she would not be slowed by formation navigation in a convoy” — which is precisely the crossing he remembers, “all by ourselves in one ship,” with everyone aboard letting out a hoot the moment the Golden Gate Bridge passed overhead. One note for the family, offered as a reading and not as evidence: the machine transcript’s nonsense phrase “we had to get for the bats and lions before the war” looks very much like the speech-recogniser mangling “Matson Lines,” which would fit his own description of a California-to-the-Pacific passenger run. Even so, without a sailing list the ship stays unnamed.

Out of the Jungle: The Markham Valley likely

There is a geographic reason Nadzab felt like relief. The Markham Valley runs roughly 160 kilometres inland from the sea at Lae, climbing about 500 metres along its length, and its floor is a flat expanse of dry kunai grass rather than rainforest — open country, hot and often bone dry, burnt off by local people once a year. Dobodura had been kunai too, but a narrow plain hemmed in by the swamp and bush behind the Buna beaches, and before that came Port Moresby’s dust; Nadzab sat in an open corridor well inland, with the jungle pushed back to the ranges on either side.

Allen’s afternoon change of air is exactly the behaviour such a corridor produces: the valley opens onto the Huon Gulf, and as the inland plain heats through the day it draws air up the valley from the sea. No period meteorological record naming that wind has been found, so the mechanism here is offered as consistent with the geography rather than as documented fact. It is worth noting that other veterans remembered Nadzab as hot, dusty and wet; Allen’s “best place we were at” is a comparison, and the thing he is comparing it to is the jungle.

Nadzab: The Jump He Arrived After

Nadzab was taken by the first major airborne assault of the Pacific war, on 5 September 1943, months before Allen’s squadron ever saw it. The US Army’s official history records an armada of 302 aircraft drawn from eight different fields: six squadrons of B-25 strafers worked the field over with nose guns and parachute fragmentation bombs, six A-20s laid smoke once the last bomb had gone off, and eighty-one C-47s carrying the 503rd Parachute Infantry Regiment were emptied in four and a half minutes, watched by Generals MacArthur and Kenney from a flight of three B-17s overhead. Australian gunners of the 2/4th Field Regiment jumped with them, and an Australian pioneer battalion that had walked and rafted in began work on the strip the same day.

Within a fortnight the engineers had two parallel 6,000-foot runways down and six more strips started. The 90th Bombardment Squadron flew in from Dobodura on 30 January 1944, with group headquarters following on 3 February — nearly five months after the drop, to a base already largely built. Secondary accounts credit the six smoke-laying A-20s to the 89th Bombardment Squadron, a sister squadron in Allen’s own group, but the official history does not name the unit and that attribution should be treated as unconfirmed.

Dobodura: “Below the Bay”

Allen’s “Dogadura” is Dobodura, a plain of kunai grass lying inland from Buna on the north-east coast of Papua rather than the east coast proper. American engineers and infantrymen began clearing there early in November 1942 to support the Buna fighting: a rough strip was usable by 21 November, and three more were finished by 12 December, one with a 4,200-foot runway. The complex kept growing — eleven interconnected strips in the main group, and by Pacific Wrecks’ count fifteen or more runways in the vicinity, many linked by taxiways — until it was one of the great Allied air-base areas of New Guinea.

Nothing about it was self-sufficient: supplies came overland from the harbour at Oro Bay, and the bay was what fed the field. Station records compiled by Pacific Wrecks place the 90th Bombardment Squadron at Horanda strip, also called Dobodura No. 4, from 21 May 1943, having moved up from Port Moresby, with group headquarters logged the day before; the standard USAAF station list gives the 3rd Bombardment Group at Dobodura from 20 May 1943. The squadron stayed there about eight months, longer than at any other New Guinea station. Allen’s “below the bay” is a fair description of the place: Oro Bay lay to the south-east.

In Memoriam

The 3rd Bomb Group's own history book closes its record of the war with a memorial roll: roughly 350 names of the group's own men who did not come home, prefaced by a short poem signed Edward Fenton — “All that I have I give. General MacArthur's Southwest Pacific Area Psychological Warfare Branch, formed in Brisbane in June 1944, produced close to 200 different leaflets in its “J1” numbered series — small codes like “25J1” or “159J1” printed directly on the leaflet identified which one it was.
