Pennsylvania National Guard

1936 – 1940, 213th Coast Artillery

Before the Air Force

Six Victories and Two Defeats

Before the armory and the drill nights there was a football field. The 1935 Maroon and White, the Cressona High School yearbook, gives the team a page and Allen a number: 3, back row, sixth from the left.

“The football season began with the same old slogan, ‘A winning team this year.’ It was an ideal but a hard one for Coach Stauffer and his boys to attempt. Thirty-two candidates reported for work and only five of them were varsity men. Everyone went to work with a will and the good old Cressona spirit. The results were most gratifying because we had a glorious beginning when we beat the Pottsville J. V’s. 19-0 and finished the season with six victories and only two defeats.”

The schedule bears it out, and so do the two that got away. Cressona 19, Pottsville J.V.s 0. Cressona 7, Schuylkill Haven 22. Cressona 12, Port Carbon 0. Cressona 20, Pine Grove 7, and 13 to 6 in the return away. Cressona 0, Cass Township 31. Cressona 7, St. Clair 0. Cressona 20, the Alumni 0. Ninety-eight points to sixty-six across the season, and both defeats heavy ones.

He turned sixteen a fortnight into that season. Two years later he would be in the National Guard for the drill pay, and three years after that a sergeant of Battery F.

Sources: Maroon and White, Cressona High School, 1935, p. 38.

The Mathematics Prize, June 1936

A school prize, four years before the uniform. The Call of Schuylkill Haven reported the Cressona High School commencement on its front page of Friday 12 June 1936: the thirty-fifth annual exercises, held in the school on the Wednesday evening, twenty-four young people taking their diplomas before a well-filled auditorium. The graduating speakers took as their subjects the different trades they expected to follow.

Then the prizes. “The Alumni Association gave the following awards to the members of the class: French and History prize to Miss Eleanore Kulp, Mathematics prize to Allen Sweigert, Science prize to Miss Evelyn Herring.”

He was seventeen. Within five years the Army would put him through twenty-two weeks of radio theory at Scott Field and he would graduate from that with high honours too; within ten he would be wiring houses and selling Philcos, and after that he spent thirty years as an electrician and an electrical foreman. The whole working life is already visible in a line of small type about a school prize.

Source: The Call, Schuylkill Haven, 12 June 1936, p. 1.

The Mathematics Prize, and Who Gave It

A second paper carries the prize, and it adds the thing the first one left out. The Call of Friday 12 June 1936, on its front page under NINETY GRADUATE: “The Alumni Association gave the following awards to the members of the class: French and History prize to Miss Eleanore Kulp; Mathematics prize to Allen Sweigert; Science prize to Miss Evelyn Herring.” The local Legion post gave the English prize, also to Miss Kulp, and Paul Strauch handed out the alumni prizes.

So the mathematics prize was the Alumni Association’s. Three years later, in June 1939, the same association sat him at its annual banquet and had him introduce the president of the senior class. He had been one of their prize-winners; then he was on the programme.

The rest of the page fills in the room. Eleanore Kulp was valedictorian and took “Teaching as a Career” for her theme; Ezebel Yankauskie was salutatorian and spoke on business; Claire Specht spoke on nursing, Evelyn Herring on home economics, Margaret Yeick on music and art. Mrs. J. W. Bittle, president of the school board, handed out the diplomas, and an orchestra under Clarke Rauch played.

Sources: The Call, Schuylkill Haven, 12 June 1936, p. 1; Pottsville Republican, 10 June 1936.

An Allen Sweigert Who Is Not This One

Every name in this chapter had to be checked rather than assumed, and one of them failed. The Lancaster New Era of Monday 18 February 1935 lists the acts in a children’s variety show — a ring master, a Midgets from Hollywood skit, an elocution turn, a Lion solo — and among the performers of a “surprise specialty from the World’s Fair”: Edna Mae Gibble, John Meeking, Nancy Biemesderfer, Suzanne Steele, Margaret Ziegler, Donald Biemesderfer and Allen Sweigert.

That is not him. Lancaster is more than fifty miles from Cressona, the surnames around him are Lancaster County surnames, and in February 1935 this Allen Sweigert was a sixteen-year-old at Cressona High School, where the same papers have him running a school assembly the previous October and speaking at a banquet the following May.

It is recorded here rather than quietly dropped because it is the shape of the problem. The name is not rare enough to stand alone. In the searches behind this chapter, a Pete Sweigert plays football at Cressona in 1937, a Bernice Sweigert goes to Girl Scout camp in 1939, a Marlin Sweigert rides the Gettysburg train in 1931, and an Allen Sweigert sings in Lancaster. Only the ones with Cressona and a date around them are treated as him.

Sources: Lancaster New Era, 18 February 1935, p. 8.

A Second Scout List, August 1932

The Gettysburg excursion of October 1931 is not the only time the Scouts put him in the paper as a boy. The Pottsville Republican of Tuesday 23 August 1932 prints another county-wide list, town by town, with a scoutmaster named at the head of one of the contingents: “From Cressona: Robert Dando, Ray Berger, Mrs. Robert Berger, Millard Wilson, Francis Moser, Henry E. Otto, Vincent Fegley, Edwin Zimmerman, Allen E. Sweigert, Richard Rubright, Dean Berger, Harry C. Jennings, Betty Berger, F. J. Jennings.”

He was thirteen, and Robert Dando heads the Cressona list again, as he had on the train to Gettysburg ten months before. What the 1932 outing was, this document cannot say: the headline sits above the part of the page the reader could reach, and the column beside it is an advertisement for a motor excursion to Atlantic City on 27 August with two nights’ lodging for $4.50, which is a neighbour on the page and not necessarily the same thing.

What it adds is continuity. Scouting has him at thirteen in 1931, at thirteen again in 1932, and running a troop of his own at twenty-one in 1939.

Sources: Pottsville Republican, 23 August 1932, p. 5; Pottsville Republican, 10 October 1931, p. 2.

Headquarters Battery, July 1937 — and a Second Sweigert

This chapter has carried a note headed “Headquarters Battery in 1937, Battery F by 1942”, worked out sideways from other evidence. Here it is said outright. The Pottsville Republican of Monday 12 July 1937 prints the roster of the men going to camp, and closes it: “Paul C. Hinkle and Floyd W. Witman have been assigned to National Guard Reserve and will proceed with the Headquarters Battery to camp on the active list.” The Call reprinted the same list four days later.

The roster runs alphabetically to the end: “…John L. Russell, Nelson Shollenberger, Joseph L. Stees, Alfred T. Stank, Allen E. Sweigert, Luther E. Sweigert, Harry W. Wentzel, Francis E. Wil[liams], Harold H. Woodring, Steven Zulick.”

Two Sweigerts, one after the other, in the same battery. Allen was eighteen and had been in the Guard about eight months on his own account. Who Luther was, this document does not say and will not guess: what it can say is that he was not a brother, because the notice of his parents’ golden wedding in 1964 states flatly that Earl and Helen had one son.

So the earliest firm date for his Guard service is now the summer camp of 1937, with Headquarters Battery, at eighteen — and the gunner’s examination in which he scored 98.5 falls two months before it, in May of the same year.

Sources: Pottsville Republican, 12 July 1937, p. 7; The Call, Schuylkill Haven, 16 July 1937, p. 5; The Call, 7 May 1937, p. 4.

Sergeant by August 1940, Six Weeks Before the Front Page

The front-page roster of 17 September 1940 prints him under Sergeants, and this chapter has leaned on it. There is an earlier one. The Pottsville Republican of Saturday 3 August 1940 lists the battery by grade and reaches Cressona: “Sgts. Kenneth E. Berger, Rayfield R. Berger, Allen E. Sweigert; Corporals John H. Leininger, Russel W. Rauch, Wm. F. Hartman; Private First Class Edwin T. Heffner; Privates Aaron E. Shirey, Wm. M. Shirey, Earl D. Rauch, Emmett H. Rauch… Robt. R. Dando, Edwin H. Zimmerman, Leroy E. Koch, Cressona.”

So the stripes are on him in print six weeks before federal service, not on the day after it. Two rosters, two papers, six weeks apart, both putting him among the sergeants — which is as much as a small county daily can be asked to give, and it is what the Republican meant when it wrote in April 1941 that “Sweigert has been a sergeant since 1939.”

Robert Dando is in this list too, as a private. He had been editor in chief of the school paper Allen wrote for in 1935 and headed the Cressona contingent on the Scout train to Gettysburg in 1931. By August 1940 they were in the same battery, and on that list three grades apart. On 27 September The Call prints a Robert R. Dando, Jr. among the sergeants of the same battery. Whether that is a promotion in the weeks between the two papers or an error in one of them, this document cannot say: the Republican’s own roster of 17 September has no Dando among the sergeants at all. The August list sets him as “Robt. R. Dando” without the “Jr.”, but this paper had already printed a Robert Dando, Jr. in the Cressona Guard contingent of August 1939, beside Allen.

Sources: Pottsville Republican, 3 August 1940, p. 2; Pottsville Republican, 17 September 1940, p. 1; Pottsville Republican, 12 April 1941, p. 4.

Gettysburg, October 1931: the First Time He Is in a Newspaper

The earliest appearance of him anywhere in print is not the mathematics prize and not the football team. It is a Boy Scout excursion. The Pottsville Republican of Saturday 10 October 1931 runs a story by John L. Conlin, headed for Gettysburg — hundreds of Boy Scouts from all over Schuylkill County leaving Pottsville on a special train, going through the Capitol at Harrisburg, and then out to the battlefield and the Lincoln monument.

Then it prints who went, town by town. “Cressona — Clayton Wenrich, Allen Sweigert, Francis Mosher, Henry E. Otto, Robert Dando, Jr., Lewis Heinbach, Harold Dieter, Marlin Sweigert, Fred Owens, Aaron Owens…” and on for another thirty names, with three or four adults among them.

He was thirteen years and one month old. Eight years later, in October 1939, the same newspaper would call him the scoutmaster of Troop 124 of Cressona and print the eighteen young people at his Hallowe’en party. He went to Gettysburg as a Scout and came back to run the troop.

Robert Dando, Jr. is on that train with him, aged about the same. Dando is editor in chief of the school paper in 1935, at the hot-dog roast on the Blue Mountain in June 1939, and away at Guard camp with him in August 1939. A Marlin Sweigert is in the Cressona list too; the relationship, if any, is not stated here and is not guessed at.

Sources: Pottsville Republican, 10 October 1931, p. 2; Pottsville Republican, 26 October 1939, p. 2.

Columbus Day, 1934: Sixteen, and In Charge

He had been in a newspaper once before, on the Boy Scout train to Gettysburg in 1931. This is the first time one has him running something. The Pottsville Republican of Thursday 11 October 1934, in the Cressona column: “Special exercises will be held in the local schools on Friday, Columbus Day. The exercises in the high school, which will be held during assembly, will be in charge of the History Club and will be in charge of Allen Sweigert.”

The sentence says it twice, clumsily, which is how you can tell the paper meant it. Then it prints the programme he had put together: “Song, ‘America the Beautiful’; scripture reading, Marie Wagner; vocal solo, Clare Wagner; Early Life of Columbus, Evelyn Herring; Columbus Voyage, Robert Dando; song, ‘Columbia The Gem of the Ocean’; Later Voyages of Columbus, Eleanore Kulp; Last Days of Columbus, Glen Mullins; song, ‘Hail Columbia Happy Land’; reading, America, by William Cullen Bryant; song, America.”

Eleven items, five speakers, four songs and a reading, in a school assembly on Friday 12 October 1934. He was sixteen years and one month old. A year later he was literary editor of the school paper; five years later he was scoutmaster of a troop; and by 1939 the Guard had made him a sergeant. This assembly is where that sequence starts, as far as anything printed can show.

Sources: Pottsville Republican, 11 October 1934, p. 5.

A Coffin into the Cressona Cemetery, February 1937

Charles W. Hartman, a retired railroader of Cressona, was buried from his son’s house on Schuylkill Street at two in the afternoon. The Pottsville Evening Republican of Wednesday 10 February 1937 reports the service and then the men who carried him: “The pall bearers were: Luther Geary, Elmer Miller, Frank Otto, Allen Sweigert, Martin McGuire and Earl Diehl.” The Rev. John Ramsey of St. James’ Episcopal, Schuylkill Haven, took the service and the grave; W. M. Armour of Orwigsburg was the undertaker.

Allen was eighteen years and five months old, and one of six men trusted with the weight of a coffin. It is a small item and it is worth keeping for one reason. The grave was in the Cressona Cemetery. Forty-five years later, in February 1982, the borough council appointed him to the commission that ran it, and he stayed on it for about twenty years. He carried a man into that ground at eighteen and helped look after it into his eighties.

Sources: Pottsville Republican, 10 February 1937, p. 12; Pottsville Republican, 5 February 1982, p. 1.

Alumni Dine, June 1939

Three years out of school, he was given a speaking part at his own high school’s alumni banquet. The Pottsville Republican of Saturday 10 June 1939, under the heading ALUMNI DINE: the Cressona High School Alumni Association held its annual banquet the previous evening in the high school, the social room hung with crepe paper streamers and the tables set with cut flowers, about ninety people present, William Boussum the toastmaster.

“Allen Sweigert introduced the president of the Senior Class, who in turn introduced all the graduates of the present year.” The main speaker was Vincent Dalton of Schuylkill Haven, class of 1914. Miss Claire Wagner accompanied the singing — the same Claire Wagner who had sung the vocal solo at his Columbus Day assembly five years before, and who would be in the party on the Blue Mountain five days later.

That week of June 1939 is about as full a picture of his twenties as the papers give. On the Friday he stood up at the alumni banquet; on the Wednesday he was at a hot-dog roast up at the Scout camp with twelve others, one of them Catherine Kline.

Sources: Pottsville Republican, 10 June 1939, p. 6.

Thanked at the Football Banquet, December 1937

He had left school a year and a half before, and he was still turning up for the team. The Pottsville Republican of Friday 10 December 1937 reports the football banquet: a ball from the season, painted red and white and lettered with the score and the players’ names, put in the school case; a decision that the team would stop having one captain and name a new one from the seniors before each game.

Then: “Appreciation was expressed for the work done by Wm. Hartman and Allen Sweigert and to Managers Emmet Rauch and Allen Gordon.” What the work was, the paper does not say — helping with the squad, the kit, the line, something that needed doing and that two young men out of school kept coming back to do.

Emmet Rauch is worth marking. Forty-five years later, when the borough council put Allen on the Cressona Cemetery commission, Emmett Rauch was on it with him. They were managing a high-school football team together in 1937 and running a graveyard together in the 1980s.

One caution about names. The seniors introduced that evening include a Pete Sweigert. He is not Allen and nothing here says who he was; Cressona had more than one Sweigert family in it.

Sources: Pottsville Republican, 10 December 1937, p. 26; Pottsville Republican, 5 February 1982, p. 1.

Sixteen Men from Cressona Went to Camp, August 1939

The Pottsville Republican of Tuesday 8 August 1939, in the Cressona column, sets down a thing nobody else in this collection records: who he served with at home. “The following men from Cressona went to camp last week with the National Guard: Aaron Shirey, Wm. Shirey, Ed. Heffner, Robert Dando, Jr., Allen Sweigert, Wm. Hartman, Rayfield Berger, Arnold Reichert, Richard Schneck, Kenneth Berger, Russell Rauch, Merritt Kramer, Kenneth Beck, Leroy Koch, Paul Hepler, and Elmer Knittle.”

Sixteen men out of one small borough, away at the annual summer camp in the last week of July or the first of August 1939. It is the only dated evidence in the collection of him at a Guard camp before the regiment was federalised, and it sits in the right year: the Republican would write in April 1941 that “Sweigert has been a sergeant since 1939.”

The names are not strangers. Robert Dando, Jr. was editor in chief of the school paper Allen wrote for in 1935 and was at the hot-dog roast on the Blue Mountain two months before this; Kenneth Beck was at that roast too. William Hartman was thanked beside Allen at the football banquet of December 1937. The same two dozen young people are in every item in this chapter, in different rooms.

The item just above it, on the same page, is the Girl Scouts going to their own camp on the Blue Mountain, with a Bernice Sweigert among them. She is not identified here and no relationship to Allen is claimed.

Sources: Pottsville Republican, 8 August 1939, p. 3; Pottsville Republican, 12 April 1941, p. 4.

Literary Editor, October 1935

In his last year at Cressona High the school started a Press Club, and the Pottsville Republican of Friday 18 October 1935 printed the whole slate. “Editor in Chief, Robert Dando; Literary Editor, Allen Sweigert; Art Editor, Leonard Matz; Business Manager, Robert Seitz; Circulation Manager, Lee Fidler; Alumni Editor, Millard Wilson; Sports Editor, Robert Seitz” — and then printers, distribution agents, typists, and a reporter for every year in the school.

He was the literary editor and not the editor in chief. The distinction is worth making because at a glance the item reads the other way: the two offices run across a line break, and a search highlight lands on “Chief, Allen” as though the words belonged together. Magnified, the sentence is unambiguous. Robert Dando had the chair; Allen had the writing.

The names around him are the names that keep coming back for the rest of the decade. Robert Dando, Leonard Matz, Robert Seitz and Lee Fidler are on this slate in 1935; Robert Seitz and Robert Dando are still with him at the hot-dog roast on the Blue Mountain in June 1939, in the party that also had Catherine Kline in it.

Sources: Pottsville Republican, 18 October 1935, p. 2; Pottsville Republican, 15 June 1939, p. 17.

Scoutmaster of Troop 124, October 1939

Seven weeks after his twenty-first birthday he was running a Boy Scout troop. The Pottsville Republican of Thursday 26 October 1939 has it in the Cressona column, under the heading “Had Hallowe’en Party”: “The Boy Scout Troop No. 124, of Cressona, Allen Sweigert, Scoutmaster, had a Hallowe’en party last evening in the basement of the Evangelical Church.”

Eighteen names follow, seven of them girls’ — Jeanne Merkle, Betty Herring, Alice Scott, Joan Mengle, Charlotte Miller, Gloria Schappel, Geraldine Becker — and then the boys, with his own name among them. The column lists the scoutmaster twice, once in charge and once in the room. A second Allen Sweigert of scouting age in a borough of under two thousand people is the harder thing to believe, and nothing turned up so far points to one.

It falls in the year the same paper would later date his stripes to. When three sergeants quit the 213th for the Air Corps in April 1941, the Republican wrote that “Sweigert has been a sergeant since 1939.” He was twenty-one, with a rank in the Guard and a troop of his own in the town.

One detail is worth leaving unsmoothed. His troop met in the basement of the Evangelical Church, and the item immediately above it on the same page is a different troop altogether — the one at St. Mark’s Reformed Church, whose scoutmaster was Glenn Ewing and who held their own Hallowe’en party at his house the same evening. St. Mark’s was Allen’s congregation for the whole of his life, the church he married in and was buried from. Cressona had two troops that autumn, and his was the one at the other church.

Sources: Pottsville Republican, 26 October 1939, p. 2, “Had Hallowe’en Party” and “Boy Scout Party”; Pottsville Republican, 12 April 1941, p. 4.

Candy, Sandwiches and Sweaters at Pottsville

The Call of Schuylkill Haven ran its own roster ten days later, on Friday 27 September 1940, carried over from page one under the jump head “Sch. Haven Gives”. What survives of the sentence the jump cuts into is a send-off: “…ettes, candy, sandwiches and sweaters were given them by the Red Cross at Pottsville.” An earlier reading of this page gave the fourth item as “others”. Enlarged, it is sweaters, broken across two lines — which is a different thing to hand a man in September. The first word is broken too, and what the page shows is “…ettes”: cigarettes is the obvious reading and it is still a reading. Then: “The members of Battery F from Schuylkill Haven and Cressona are as follows:” Only the jump head and the page-four text have been read. The front-page headline and the paragraphs above the jump have not.

The two lists do not agree. On Allen they do: both papers put him among the sergeants. Below that they part. The Call gives seven sergeants from Schuylkill Haven and Cressona alone: “Sergeants — Geo. A. Fatkin, John H. Roeder, John E. Dewald, Schuylkill Haven; Rayfield R. Berger, Robert R. Dando, Jr., Allen E. Sweigert and Russell W. Rauch, Cressona.” The Republican, ten days earlier, gave five for the whole battery, and its five include Edward J. Weiser of Schuylkill Haven, whom The Call does not put among its sergeants.

Dewald, Dando and Rauch are sergeants in one paper and not in the other; Weiser is a sergeant in the other and not in this one. Scope does not explain it. The Call keeps to the two towns — that is why it prints only Frank Knarr of the battery’s three second lieutenants and leaves out Rehrig of Allentown and Zerbey of Pottsville — but Weiser is a Schuylkill Haven man and belongs inside that rule. Two of the seven stood lower in the Republican’s list of 3 August: a Robt. R. Dando among the Cressona privates, a Russel W. Rauch among its corporals. Kenneth E. Berger, a Cressona sergeant on that same August list, is among the sergeants of neither September roster. Which paper had the armory’s list right cannot be settled here.

Two things the item will not carry. The privates break off into damaged type partway through, so nothing can be said about who is missing from the lower grades, and nobody can be shown absent from a list that was not read to its end. And the paper does not date the send-off: the Red Cross met them at Pottsville, the regiment went south to Camp Pendleton on 24 September by the unit records, and this note does not join the two.

Sources: The Call, Schuylkill Haven, 27 September 1940, p. 1, continued p. 4 (jump head and p. 4 text only); Pottsville Republican, 17 September 1940, p. 1; Pottsville Republican, 3 August 1940, p. 2.

The Roster, on the Front Page

The Pottsville Republican put Battery F on its front page on Tuesday 17 September 1940 — the day after federal service began — and printed every man in it. The paper made a point of the towns: “so that the two units here are not really Pottsville’s but may truly be called Schuylkill County’s First Defenders.”

Battery F, as the paper set it. Captain, H. R. D. Schwenk, Sch. Haven. First Lieutenant, Lewis R. Noecker, Sch. Haven. Second Lieutenants, Frank Knarr, Cressona; Lester Rehrig, Allentown; Joseph H. Zerbey, 3rd, Pottsville. First Sergeant, Donald L. Eiler, Sch. Haven. Staff Sergeant, Harold B. Ney, Sch. Haven. Sergeants: Rayfield R. Berger, Cressona; Geo. A. Fatkin, Sch. Haven; John H. Roeder, Sch. Haven; Allen E. Sweigert, Cressona; Edward J. Weiser, Sch. Haven.

One of five sergeants as the Republican set them, at twenty-two, on the front page of the county daily. The Call counted differently ten days later, and the note on that paper takes it up. The captain’s name is the one already written in his own service record book. And it settles something this chapter had to leave open: a 1937 newspaper puts him in Headquarters Battery, while this puts him in Battery F three years later, so he moved between them at some point before the call-up rather than being a different man altogether.

Sources: Pottsville Republican, 17 September 1940, p. 1.

A Used Car, February 1940

The smallest thing the search turned up, and worth keeping for that reason. On Friday 16 February 1940 the Pottsville Republican printed its weekly round-up of property and vehicle transfers across Schuylkill County. Under the heading “Used Car Sales were as follows”, between George Sterner of Schuylkill Haven and John Hiler of Harrisburg, is “Allen E. Sweigert, Cressona.”

He was twenty-one, four years into the Guard, eighteen months from Scott Field and two years from Australia, and he bought himself a second-hand car. The paper does not say what it was.

Sources: Pottsville Republican, 16 February 1940, p. 2.

Three Sergeants Gave Up Their Stripes

The Pottsville Republican of Saturday 12 April 1941 ran it under a headline: 3 QUIT 213TH FOR AIR CORPS, and beneath that, Sgts. Sweigert, Frack, Fedorchak Now at Savanaah Air Base — the paper’s own misspelling. It is datelined Camp Stewart and bylined Harvey D. Haddad, who writes “our induction last September” and so was in the battery himself.

“Three of Battery F’s sergeants received their discharge papers tonight, so they could enlist in the morning in the Army Air Corps and will be stationed at the air base in Savannah, Ga. The three men are Sergt. Allen E. Sweigert, Cressona; Sergt. Jesse D. Frack, Frackville, and Sergt. Andrew Fedorchak, Pottsville.”

Two things in that are new. He did not go alone — two other men went with him, and they can be named. And he was a sergeant already. The paper says so twice: “Sweigert has been a sergeant since 1939.” Enlisting in the Air Corps meant surrendering that, which is why he sailed for Australia in the ranks and why he could write home from Brisbane that he had had two promotions since leaving the States. He had been a sergeant, given it up, and had to earn it again.

The last line of the item says what he was after. “While Frack is mainly interested in the flying end of the Air Corps, the other two men are looking at the radio part of this branch.” In April 1941, before Scott Field and before anybody assigned him anything, radio was the thing he went for.

One caution. The item says the three had “been members since 1939,” where this chapter has him joining in late 1936 on his own account. Whether the writer meant Battery F specifically, or the 1939 date is simply wrong, the page does not say, and the two are left standing side by side.

Sources: Pottsville Republican, 12 April 1941, p. 4.

Headquarters Battery in 1937, Battery F by 1942

The battery he joined in late 1936 has always been given as Battery F at Pottsville, and for the later years the evidence is good. His own letter of May 1942 sends “regards to the boys of Btry. ‘F’, 213th,” and the Pottsville Republican says he “left Pottsville with the 213th Coast Artillery.”

The first two years are less settled. The Call of 7 May 1937 prints Allen E. Sweigert among the results of a gunner’s examination sat by “the men of Headquarters Battery” of the regiment. The roster of July 1937 puts him in Headquarters Battery by name, and the front page of 17 September 1940, printed above, puts him in Battery F, so he moved between them at some point between that examination and the call-up rather than being a different Allen E. Sweigert; when the move came is not recorded. The same paragraph carries a Luther M. Sweigert, same battery, same mark, with no word of how the two men were related, or whether they were at all. For 1936 and 1937, then, the safest thing to say is simply the regiment.

Sources: The Call, Schuylkill Haven, 7 May 1937, p. 4; Letter of Sgt. Allen E. Sweigert, 19 May 1942, in The Call, 12 June 1942; Pottsville Republican, 28 May 1942, p. 18.

Before the Army Air Force there were four years in the Pennsylvania National Guard — weekend drills at the armory and summer encampments in the field, from 1936 to 1940. This was Allen's first soldiering.

“The First Defenders”

The nickname predates the 213th itself. When Lincoln called for troops on April 15, 1861, five Pennsylvania militia companies reached Washington, D.C. within two days — among the first armed Union units to arrive, earning the state's volunteers the name “First Defenders.” Two of those companies were Pottsville's own: the Washington Artillerists and the National Light Infantry. In 1922 those two units were consolidated into a new Pennsylvania National Guard regiment, the 213th Coast Artillery (Anti-Aircraft), carrying the First Defenders name and lineage forward — the regiment, and Battery F, that Allen joined in 1936. After Allen left the regiment in April 1941 — discharged at his own request, and enlisted in the Air Corps at Savannah — the 213th itself deployed overseas as part of Operation Torch, fighting in North Africa and Italy, before being broken up in an April 1944 reorganization of anti-aircraft units.

Sources: Pennsylvania First Defenders; Schuylkill County Historical Society; 213th Coast Artillery Regiment archives.

Five Frames That Were in the Wrong Chapter

Five photographs printed here were, until this revision, in the Pacific chapter, filed with the camps at Port Moresby and Dobodura. They belong with the Guard. Jeremy said so, and once said it is obvious: stovepipes through the peaks of the tents, wool shirts, garrison caps, and in one of them a woman visiting in a fur-collared winter coat. Nobody wore wool or lit a tent stove in New Guinea, and no wives came to visit.

The roll agrees. All five carry frame numbers from the same strip as eighteen other pictures that were already in this chapter — the company street with the letter F, the mobile gun mount, the hand-lettered sign reading “SGT. R. BUBECK” and “SGT. A. SWEIGERT.” The five that had drifted were separated from their own strip, not from their own place. The last two are the plainest of all: one is a room with bare bulbs and a slatted window, and the other has pine woods, wooden camp buildings and a parked American sedan of the middle thirties in it.

Allen is in three of the five, which means that for those he was not the one holding the camera. Somebody in the same camp took them and he kept the prints. The galvanised bucket by the washing line is stencilled for a different sergeant, a name ending in ¹ald¹, which is the sort of detail that says whose company street this was as clearly as any caption.

Identification: Jeremy Sweigert, 24 September 2026.

Extra Money in the Coal Country

Cressona sits in the southern anthracite field of Schuylkill County, where the Depression arrived early and never really left. Between 1926 and 1933 the coal operators cut production by a third and laid off nearly 67,000 of the region’s 169,000 anthracite miners; by 1932 roughly 39,000 of Schuylkill County’s 82,000 workers were out of a job, and by the mid-1930s illegal “bootleg” mining on company land accounted for about a tenth of all anthracite produced.

That is the town Allen graduated into in the spring of 1936, and it explains why a National Guard enlistment read as wages rather than patriotism. The National Defense Act set the rhythm: a Guard battery had to turn out for drill at least forty-eight times a year and spend at least fifteen days in the field, and a Guardsman drew pay for every one of those days at a day’s rate of the Regular Army base pay for his grade. A private’s base pay in the late 1930s was $21 a month, so a drill night was worth cents rather than dollars — but it was cash, it came on a schedule, and in Schuylkill County in 1937 very little else did.

Sources: Schuylkill County [Great Depression] Historical Marker — ExplorePAHistory (Pennsylvania Historical & Museum Commission); 32 U.S.C. 502: Required drills and field exercises; 1922–1940 Enlisted Military Base Pay Chart.

Battery F, Pottsville — The First Defenders

The regiment Allen joined was the 213th Coast Artillery (Antiaircraft), a Pennsylvania National Guard unit with its headquarters at Allentown and its batteries scattered through the armories of eastern Pennsylvania: the searchlight battery at Reading, three-inch antiaircraft gun batteries at Allentown, Bethlehem and Easton, and the machine-gun batteries at Lancaster, Pottsville, Reading and Lebanon. Battery F was the Pottsville battery, working .30-calibre water-cooled guns a few miles up the Schuylkill from Cressona — near enough that a boy from Cressona could belong to it.

The regiment’s motto, “The First Defenders,” was earned rather than chosen: of the five Pennsylvania militia companies that answered Lincoln’s call in April 1861 and became the first volunteer troops to reach Washington, at six in the evening on 18 April, two — the Washington Artillerists and the National Light Infantry — were Pottsville companies, and the 213th descended from them by way of the 4th Pennsylvania Infantry. Both came down into the regiment as its Service and Headquarters batteries, so when Allen turned out for weekly drill in the Pottsville armory in 1937 he was standing in a line — his town’s and his regiment’s both — that ran back to the first Union volunteers to reach the capital. The squadron patches and unit names of the Pacific were still five years away; this was his first outfit, and it was a local one.

Sources: FIRST DEFENDERS — The 213th Coast Artillery Regiment (Anti Aircraft) — unit history site listing battery home stations and armament; 213th Air Defense Artillery Regiment (United States) — lineage of the 213th Coast Artillery (AA); Pennsylvania First Defenders; Historical Sketches: Coast Artillery Regiments 1917–1950 (National Guard) — Coast Defense Study Group.

“Something Brewing”: The New York Manoeuvres

Allen sets this camp “before we were mobilized,” which places it in the summer of 1940 rather than 1941 — by the summer of 1941 he had already left the Guard and was at radio school in Illinois. From 5 to 23 August 1940 the First Army held its manoeuvres in northern New York, putting provisional I, II and III Corps of Regular Army and National Guard troops into the field through St. Lawrence County, with the camps spread around Canton and Ogdensburg — by far the largest body of troops most of these men had ever stood in.

The county history of the 213th records the regiment at III Corps manoeuvres at Heuvelton, near Ogdensburg, and a citation from 28th Division headquarters during the First Army manoeuvres for its “high spirit and usual initiative” and the manner in which it gave antiaircraft protection. Three weeks in the field instead of the customary fifteen days, camped alongside the regular establishment, with France newly fallen and the newspapers full of it — the feeling that something was brewing was entirely sound. The men came out of the field on 23 August. The call-up order reached them three weeks later, on 15 September.

Sources: Schuylkill County Pennsylvania Military History: The 213th Coast Artillery, Schuylkill County Men During the Years of Peace, 1922–1941; First Army Maneuvers: Northern New York, August 5 to 23, 1940 (contemporary official publication); Biennial Reports of the Chief of Staff of the United States Army, July 1, 1939 to June 30, 1941 (HyperWar); 44th Infantry Division (United States) — First Army manoeuvres near Canton, New York, August 1940.

16 September 1940: A Camp in Virginia

Congress opened the door with Joint Resolution 96, approved 27 August 1940, which authorised the President to order the National Guard into federal service for twelve consecutive months — and forbade its use beyond the Western Hemisphere and America’s own possessions. The first Guard units went in on 16 September 1940, the same day the Selective Training and Service Act was signed into law; in all, the Guard handed the Army 20,006 officers, 216 warrant officers and 282,805 enlisted men.

The 213th was in that first wave, inducted at Allentown on 15 September and moved to Camp Pendleton, Virginia, on 24 September for training, then on to Camp Stewart, Georgia, on 22 February 1941 — Camp Stewart being the Army’s brand-new antiaircraft training centre, carved out of nearly half of Liberty County and pieces of four more, raw pine and palmetto scrub where live antiaircraft firing did not begin until July 1941. He had the order of it exactly right — a camp in Virginia, then Camp Stewart — and only the length of time generous, since the regiment had been in Georgia barely seven weeks when he was discharged on 10 April 1941. The twelve-month promise did not hold either: on 12 August 1941 the House voted 203 to 202 to extend service by up to eighteen months more, and the buddies he went back to visit on 7 December — the regiment held Camp Stewart until the 12th — were still in uniform because of a single vote.

Res. 222, August 12, 1941 — U.S. Capitol Visitor Center.

Getting Out of a Federalised Regiment likely

A man could be let out of a federalised Guard regiment before his twelve months ran; which paragraph of the rules let Allen out, nobody now knows. The governing regulation was AR 615-360, Enlisted Personnel — Discharge, and its Section X covered discharge for the convenience of the Government. Regular Army enlistment, meanwhile, stayed open through 1941 and was suspended for the duration on 13 December of that year — eight months after Allen walked through that door.

Other Guardsmen in federal service certainly did take Regular Army enlistments. A Comptroller General decision of 22 December 1941 turns on a man discharged from the 120th Engineers on 16 September 1941 who enlisted in the Regular Army the next day — but his term had simply run out, which is not what the notices describe for Allen. They describe a request granted in the middle of one. No War Department instruction allowing a man out for the express purpose of joining the Air Corps has ever turned up.

Sources: Comptroller General decision B-21629, 22 December 1941, 21 Comp. Gen. 603; AR 615-360, Enlisted Personnel — Discharge; Army of the United States — Wikipedia.

He Asked To Be Let Go

Until these two notices surfaced, the only account of how Allen left the 213th was his own, given on tape sixty-four years afterwards. Both papers, set from a family’s information within months of the event, say the same thing and add one word that changes the shape of it. “He left Pottsville with the 213th Coast Artillery and later asked for a discharge, which was granted, and then he enlisted in the regular army with the Air Corps at Savannah, Georgia.” The other: “Some time later, he asked for his release which was granted and he then enlisted in the regular Army Air Corps at Savannah, Ga.”

Two compositors, working from separate copy, set the same two steps in the same order: a discharge he asked for, and then a fresh enlistment. So it was not a transfer at all. And the thing the papers add — the thing no other source in the collection says — is that the asking was his.

Sources: Pottsville Republican, 28 May 1942, p. 18; The Call, Schuylkill Haven, 29 May 1942, p. 1; transcript-part1.html.

What the “20” in His Serial Number Means

Army serial number 20 329 905 is not a random string; in the wartime system the leading digits told a clerk where a man had come from. A number beginning with 1 belonged to a Regular Army volunteer, 3 or 4 to a man who had been drafted, and 20 to a federally recognised National Guardsman.

Because every Guard number carried a zero in the second place, the geography was pushed to the third digit — and 3 was the Third Corps Area, headquartered at Baltimore and covering Pennsylvania, Maryland, Virginia and the District of Columbia. So the number on Allen’s papers states, on its face and without any family testimony at all, that he entered federal service as a Pennsylvania National Guardsman. It stayed with him through the discharge in Georgia, the enlistment at Savannah, three years of New Guinea and the discharge at Fort Dix: whatever the shoulder patch said afterwards, the first two digits always said Guard.

Sources: How to Decode a WWII US Army Serial Number — Amy Johnson Crow; Army Serial Numbers (ASN) history — WWII Dog Tags; Service number (United States Army) — Wikipedia (20-million series, National Guard); Corps area — Wikipedia (Third Corps Area: Pennsylvania, Maryland, District of Columbia and Virginia; headquartered at Baltimore).

The Radio School at Scott Field

Scott Field, outside Belleville, Illinois, had spent the 1920s and 1930s as the Army’s lighter-than-air station, dominated by an airship hangar three blocks long and fifteen storeys high; the balloon and dirigible era ended abruptly in 1937, and on 1 June 1939 the field turned formally back to teaching as a branch of the Air Corps Technical Schools. Radio instruction had been concentrated at Chanute Field, but when Chanute’s space was needed for aeroplane mechanics the radio work was moved to Scott, where the Radio School opened in September 1940.

Allen would have sat through the radio operator-mechanic course: twenty-two weeks long as it stood in 1940, meant to turn out a man who could work an aircraft radio station at sixteen words a minute and do first and second echelon maintenance on all of its equipment. His own dates, “maybe June up to about October,” fit that course almost exactly — and he arrived with a head start, having already been working through a mail-order radio course at home in Cressona before the Army had any use for it. By the end of the war Scott Field had graduated 77,370 radio operator-mechanics and grown into some forty-six school buildings; Allen went through it in the school’s first year.

Sources: The Army Air Forces in World War II, Volume VI: Men and Planes, Chapter 19 (HyperWar) — radio operator-mechanic course, 22 weeks, 16 words per minute; transfer of radio training from Chanute to Scott; Scott Air Force Base — Wikipedia (lighter-than-air era, Radio School, 77,370 graduates); Scott History: 1940s — Scott Air Force Base official history.

Savannah Air Base Was Very Nearly Its Name

The field at Savannah carried two names at once, and the notices used the Army’s. The city bought the land in 1927 and dedicated the airport as Hunter Field in May 1940, after a Savannah-born flying ace of the first war. On 30 August 1940 the Air Corps received approval to build a base there, and the airfield was dedicated as Savannah Army Air Base on 19 February 1941 — a few weeks before Allen enlisted into it. It became Hunter Air Force Base in September 1950.

So “Savannah Air Base” is not loose home-town shorthand for an unnamed field. It is the installation’s own designation, missing only the word Army. Within about two months of the 1940 decision the Air Corps had moved some three thousand men of the 3rd and 27th Bombardment Groups there, and Camp Stewart, where the 213th was then sitting, is forty miles away. The enlistment was a short journey, not a leap.

Sources: Hunter Army Airfield — unit and installation histories; New Georgia Encyclopedia, Camp Stewart.

Where the Two Papers Disagree

The notices were set from the same news and diverge all through, but on one point of substance they contradict each other. The first has him returning to Savannah Air Base “from where he left shortly for overseas to Australia.” The second: he “returned to Savannah Air Base and some time later left for Australia.”

The calendar settles it against the first. He graduated at Scott Field on 27 October 1941, by his own account; the 3rd Bombardment Group left Savannah on 19 January 1942 and sailed from San Francisco on the 31st. That is twelve weeks exactly, which is not “shortly.” The looser wording is the accurate one, and the distinction earns its place because of what falls inside the interval: the Carolina Maneuvers of 16-28 November 1941, the episode this document has from the back of his own photograph. The first paper’s compression would leave no room for it.

Sources: Pottsville Republican, 28 May 1942, p. 18; The Call, Schuylkill Haven, 29 May 1942, p. 1; the-record.html, Convoy 2030.

Twenty-Two Weeks, Now From the Family’s Own Hand

The length of his course had to be worked out the long way round, from the official history. His parents simply knew it. Both 1942 papers state the figure outright, in the family’s own words: “to school for 22 weeks to learn to be a radio operator.” That the number is reported rather than deduced matters, because the same official history records that soon after Pearl Harbor the radio operator-mechanic curriculum was cut to eighteen weeks. His was the long version, and that puts his class before December 1941.

It dates him, too, though not in the direction anyone expected. Working only from the twenty-two weeks, the arithmetic once put the start of the course in the first half of June 1941, which made his remembered “about October” look a few weeks early. Then his own letter of 19 May 1942 gave the date outright: he graduated at Scott Field on 27 October 1941. Twenty-two weeks back from that lands about 26 May, six weeks after he enlisted at Savannah. So the course began in late May. His memory was right and the arithmetic was wrong, and he was back with the squadron about three weeks before the manoeuvres opened on 16 November.

Sources: Pottsville Republican, 28 May 1942, p. 18; The Call, Schuylkill Haven, 29 May 1942, p. 1; Craven & Cate, The Army Air Forces in World War II, Vol. VI, ch. 19.

Cressona in 1936

Cressona was settled in 1847 and incorporated as a borough ten years later, named for John Chapman Cresson, the Philadelphia engineer who managed the Schuylkill Navigation Company and presided over the Mine Hill and Schuylkill Haven Railroad; the borough grew up along that line, four miles south of Pottsville, and has never covered much more than a square mile. When Allen finished high school in 1936 it held fewer than two thousand people and was losing them — 1,946 at the 1930 census, 1,695 by 1940. Behind that drop was the collapse of the anthracite trade.

Between 1926 and 1933 the operators cut production by a third and let go nearly 67,000 of the region’s 169,000 miners, and Schuylkill County took the worst of it: by 1932 roughly 39,000 of the county’s 82,000 workers were out of work, 16,000 of them miners. Men dug coal illegally out of company ground to eat, and as late as 1941 bootleg mining still accounted for about one job in four in Schuylkill and Northumberland Counties. Against a background like that, a National Guard drill cheque was real money, and “I did get some part-time jobs” is the whole of the local economy in one clause.

Sources: Cressona, Pennsylvania — census figures, founding and namesake (Wikipedia); Cressona Borough, Munsell’s History of Schuylkill County (USGenWeb Archives transcription); ExplorePAHistory — “Bootleg Coal Mining” historical marker essay (unemployment and production figures for Schuylkill County); Schuylkill County [Great Depression] Historical Marker — ExplorePAHistory (production, layoff and unemployment figures verified verbatim).

Battery F was a Pottsville outfit likely

The battery Allen joined in late 1936 drilled four miles up the road: Battery F of the 213th Coast Artillery (Anti-Aircraft) was based at Pottsville, which is why a Cressona boy could belong to it and still sleep at home between weekly drills. The regiment carried the motto “The First Defenders,” and the claim was literal.

When Lincoln called for troops on 15 April 1861, five Pennsylvania companies reached Washington before any others, arriving on the 18th — and two of them, the Washington Artillerists and the National Light Infantry, were Pottsville men. In the 1922 reorganisation those two became the Service Battery and the Headquarters Battery of the regiment that took the name 213th Coast Artillery two years later, so the anti-aircraft regiment recruiting in Schuylkill County in the 1930s held one of the oldest defence lineages in the United States. The mobilisation date reaches us a day apart, depending on who wrote it down: one regimental account gives 15 September 1940, the family record 16 September 1940.

Sources: FIRST DEFENDERS — The 213th Coast Artillery Regiment (Anti Aircraft): 1940 battery locations, Battery F at Pottsville; The 213th Coast Artillery: Schuylkill County Men During the Years of Peace, 1922–1941 (Schuylkill County Military History); Pennsylvania First Defenders (Wikipedia) — the five 1861 companies; 213th Air Defense Artillery Regiment (Wikipedia) — regimental lineage and motto.

The paper trail that matches his caption

For years the November 1941 maneuvers were the one episode in Allen’s account that could not be matched to a printed page. The standard unit histories of the 3rd Bombardment Group record only that it sat at Savannah, Georgia, from 6 October 1940 to 19 January 1942, and say nothing at all about North Carolina. The order of battle for the exercise itself does. Listed under the Blue force’s 1st Air Support Command, in both phases of the maneuver, is the 3d Bombardment Group — Allen’s own group, lent out for the length of the war game to Colonel William E. Kepner’s command and to Lieutenant General Hugh Drum’s First Army.

Its parent unit went the other way. The 3rd Air Support Command, activated on 1 September 1941 at the Army Air Base at Savannah — Allen’s own station — served the Red side under IV Corps. The Army’s own history of Hunter Field adds that the 3rd and 27th Bomb Groups trained there through 1940 and 1941 while taking part in large-scale Army maneuvers in the Carolinas. No published source names the squadron or the airfield. For those two things the only witness is Allen’s own handwriting.

Sources: 3rd Air Support Command (activation, assignment dates, attachment to IV Corps); History of Hunter Army Airfield, U.S. Army Garrison Fort Stewart; 3rd Bombardment Group, historyofwar.org (stations and dates); 3rd Bombardment Group, Peter Dunn’s Australia @ War.

The largest war game the Army had ever run

The exercise Allen joined covered more ground than any war game the United States Army had ever fought on its own soil, though not more men — the Louisiana maneuvers of that same autumn had fielded some 400,000. Roughly 350,000 contended here, across a box about ninety by a hundred and fifty miles south and east of Charlotte, on the Charlotte-Columbia-Camden-Cheraw-Fort Bragg line. It ran in two phases: the first from 16 to 21 November 1941, remembered afterward as the Battle of the Pee Dee, and the second from 25 to 28 November, the Second Battle of Camden.

Lieutenant General Hugh Drum’s Blue First Army of about 195,000 met a smaller Red force of roughly 100,000 under Major General Oscar Griswold, stiffened by the I Armored Corps and George Patton’s tanks. Each side was given an air support command of its own, and on the opening day of the second phase Blue aviation — Allen’s side of the game — bombed the Pee Dee bridges from Cheraw south. The whole thing was called off on 28 November. Pearl Harbor came nine days later.

Sources: “Relax — It’s Only a Maneuver”, HistoryNet (phase dates, force sizes, area, air action, end date); Carolina Maneuvers (order of battle, troop strength, Red and Blue air support commands); 1st Air Support Command (Kepner, attachment to First Army); The U.S. Army GHQ Maneuvers of 1941, CMH Pub 70-41 (Gabel), U.S. Army Center of Military History.

Canvas in the longleaf pines likely

The picture delivers what the caption promises: tents pitched among tall pines, a group of men standing about in the sand. Canvas was the whole of the arrangement. An army on maneuvers bivouacked where it stopped, tens of miles from any barracks, and November in the sandhills is not mild — men who were on the Carolina Maneuvers remembered ice on the water buckets in the morning and a scramble for kerosene heaters, with early C-rations for chow.

The permanent post behind them was bursting all the same. Fort Bragg had held 5,406 officers and enlisted men on 1 June 1940 and carried more than 67,000 by 1941, and in a single nine-month stretch of those two years it went from 376 buildings of various types to 3,155 — the scale of the expansion that had put Allen in uniform in the first place. Out in the field, letters still found them — mail call was as welcome there as anywhere, handed round by the company clerk or a non-commissioned officer instead of collected at an orderly-room window back in garrison. It is a cold camp in that picture, whatever else it is.

Sources: “Relax — It’s Only a Maneuver”, HistoryNet (field conditions, ice on the water buckets, rations); Fort Bragg, North Carolina Postal History Society (garrison strength, 376 to 3,155 buildings, mail call in the field); Pope AFB (Pope Field), NC — History (Fort Bragg strength 1940-41, paved runways 1940).

Marston Mat was born in the same weeks, a few miles away

Within the same maneuver area, in the same month, the Army laid the first pierced-steel-plank airstrip ever built in the United States. The site was a low hill a mile east of US Route 1, two miles northeast of the hamlet of Marston, North Carolina, southwest of Fort Bragg and beside what would later be Camp Mackall’s airfield. Engineers cleared and graded 350 feet by 3,800 feet, moved some fifty thousand cubic yards of earth and laid a strip 150 feet wide and 3,000 feet long, and the whole job took eleven days; each panel was ten feet by fifteen inches, weighed 66.2 pounds, and needed no tool but a sledge to hook it to its neighbor.

It came up as easily as it went down — when the maneuvers ended the strip was lifted, loaded into eighteen railroad gondola cars and hauled off to Langley Field, Virginia. Judged a success, the mat went to the Pacific carrying the name of that North Carolina hamlet, and Allen would meet it again on the New Guinea strips. Nothing in his account says he saw it laid, and there is no reason to suppose he did. But the steel he later took entirely for granted and the tent camp in his photograph belong to the same few weeks and very nearly the same stretch of Carolina pine.

Sources: Marston Mat (name origin, 3,000 by 150 foot strip, eleven days, panel dimensions and weight, Pacific use); “Marston Mat”, Air & Space Forces Magazine (site description, construction figures, earthmoving).

The sister group that went the other way

Two light bombardment groups shared Hunter Field at Savannah in 1941, the 3rd and the 27th, carried on the rolls of the same air support command — the 27th with A-24 dive bombers, four of the 3rd’s five squadrons with A-20s. In November they parted. The 27th sailed from San Francisco on 1 November 1941 and its ground echelon reached Manila on the 20th; the ship carrying its A-24s, the Meigs, could be found no escort past Hawaii before 24 November, and the convoy was turned for Australia.

The planes never reached the Philippines, and the men who were waiting for them fought as infantry on Bataan and Corregidor, most of them into captivity. The 27th’s assignment to the 3rd Air Support Command lapsed around 20 November, the very week Allen’s group was in the Carolina pines. The two outfits met again all the same: in March 1942, 42 officers, 62 enlisted men and 24 of those A-24s were assigned to Allen’s 3rd Bombardment Group. Nothing in the interview suggests he knew at the time which way the two had been sent, or what the difference would come to mean.

Sources: 27th Bombardment / 27th Fighter Group, historyofwar.org (sailed 1 Nov 1941, Manila 20 Nov 1941, aircraft never delivered); 27th Bombardment Group, Peter Dunn’s Australia @ War; 3rd Air Support Command (27th Bombardment Group assigned 1 Sep to c. 20 Nov 1941); History of Hunter Army Airfield, U.S. Army Garrison Fort Stewart (3rd and 27th Bomb Groups at Hunter, 1940-41).
