The Unit History Book

Allen kept his own copy of the 3rd Bomb Group's unit history book, The Reaper's Harvest — its cover carries the same blue oval emblem used throughout this document, and gold-embossed B-25 silhouettes in the corners.

The 90th Squadron

The 90th Squadron's Own “The Commanders” Page

Not one of Allen's own photographs — this page, too, comes from the 90th Squadron's own chapter of The Reaper's Harvest, naming the squadron's commanding officers “past and present” as of the book's printing: Maj. Stanley D. Kline (commanding), Maj. C. D. Prokopovitz (ex-commander), Capt. W. T. Robinson (executive officer), and Maj. K. E. Rosebush (ex-commander). None of these four names match the nine commanding officers Allen himself listed by surname in his own service record book (Wilson, Hubbard, Dickerson, Peterson, Ellis, Lasner, Hendry, Kretzsch, Priboroski) — likely because the book's list reflects who held squadron command near the end of the war, while Allen's own list spans commanders he served under earlier in his tour.

The 3rd Bomb Group — “The Grim Reapers”

The 3rd Bombardment Group deployed to Australia in early 1942 and flew under the newly formed Fifth Air Force out of Port Moresby by January 1943, with five squadrons: HQ, 8th, 13th, 89th, and 90th. In late 1942, engineering officer Maj. Paul “Pappy” Gunn modified the group's B-25s at Eagle Farm, Brisbane — stripping the glass bombardier nose and packing it with four fixed .50-cal guns, plus twin “package guns” on each side, cleared to attack at masthead height. The entire 90th Squadron was flying these “commerce destroyer” strafers by February 1943. At the Battle of the Bismarck Sea (2–3 March 1943) — the first sea-level B-25 strafing attack of the war — the 90th hit every ship in an 18-vessel Japanese convoy. The group struck Wewak's airfields on 17 August 1943, earning a Distinguished Unit Citation, then staged from Dobodura and Nadzab as the front moved forward through 1943–44.

Sources: Army Air Corps Museum; OzAtWar field-modification records.

The Group's Medal of Honor

The 3rd Bomb Group's own history book records one Medal of Honor, earned in the group's costliest raid: the low-level strike on Simpson Harbor, Rabaul, November 2, 1943, that sank 104,000 tons of Japanese shipping but flew the Mitchells directly into the harbor's ring of naval anti-aircraft fire. Major Raymond H. Wilkins, his own bomber already damaged, deliberately flew it into the heaviest fire from two Japanese heavy cruisers — drawing their guns onto himself and away from the rest of his squadron so they could get through. He did not survive the mission. The book's own words: “He sacrificed his life to save the aircraft of his squadron.” It isn't recorded which of the group's squadrons Wilkins himself flew with, so this is offered as the wider unit's history, not a claim that Allen's own 90th Squadron was part of that specific raid.

Source: The Reaper's Harvest, 3rd Attack Group Association.

The Planes He Photographed

Serial numbers and fates for the named B-25s in Allen's own collection, cross-referenced against the 3rd Bomb Group's surviving loss records and its own official unit history, The Reaper's Harvest — the same book Allen kept his copy of, digitized in full by the 3rd Attack Group Association.

Spook II, 41-12969, was Larner's own aircraft. Leading his squadron into the attack at the Battle of the Bismarck Sea, March 3, 1943, he radioed his wingmen off his tail — “Get off my wing, you guys, get your own damn ships! This one's mine!” — and bore down alone on the largest ship in the convoy, a Teratsuki-class destroyer. The 3rd Bomb Group's strafers helped sink or damage every vessel in an 18-ship convoy that day, the action that proved low-level strafing tactics could work. Spook II (also logged as Onkle Rafe) was flown by a pilot named Larner until it crashed April 30, 1943.

Fat Cat, 41-12449, was flown by 1st Lt. William R. “Red” Johnson. Feather Merchant (also called Irene), 41-12442 — likely the “Merchant” in Allen's own photo — survived the war and was later put on display at Aitape High School. Chatter Box is harder to pin down: one detailed account gives 41-13088, piloted by Capt. Bob Chatt at the Bismarck Sea; the 3rd Bomb Group's own loss roster instead lists a “El Diablo III / Chatter Box,” 41-30088, pilot Howe, force-landed April 16, 1943 — possibly the same aircraft renamed, possibly two planes that shared a name, as squadrons sometimes did once one was lost. No serial number turned up for Hell's Fire, Early Bird, Shy-Baby, Fat Cat II, or Sad Sack despite searching both the unit's own history book and outside aviation archives; a data plate or nose number in a clearer photo would resolve them.

Sources: Pacific Wrecks, 3rd Bombardment Group aircraft roster; worldwarphotos.info; Warbird Information Exchange forum; The Reaper's Harvest (3rd Attack Group Association).

Chatter Box, In the Flesh

Not one of Allen's own photographs — this comes from the same unit history book, and it puts a face on the aircraft discussed on the previous page: the 90th Squadron's own Chatter Box, shark's teeth painted across her nose, with the men of the squadron posed in front of her. The book's own caption reads simply, “90th Squadron men who participated in Bismarck Sea Battle.” Allen himself isn't identified in the crowd, and the photo doesn't resolve which of the two logged serial numbers this particular airframe carried — but it confirms the name, the nose art, and that the squadron considered this photograph significant enough to print.
