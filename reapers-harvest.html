Allen kept his own copy of the 3rd Bomb Group's unit history book, The Reaper's Harvest — its cover carries the same blue oval emblem that runs through these pages, and gold-embossed B-25 silhouettes in the corners.

The 90th Squadron

The 90th Squadron's Own “The Commanders” Page

Another page from the 90th Squadron's own chapter of The Reaper's Harvest, naming the squadron's commanding officers “past and present” as of the book's printing: Maj. Stanley D. Kline (commanding), Maj. C. D. Prokopovitz (ex-commander), Capt. W. T. Robinson (executive officer), and Maj. K. E. Rosebush (ex-commander). Not one of those four names appears among the nine commanding officers Allen wrote out by surname in his own service record book (Wilson, Hubbard, Dickerson, Peterson, Ellis, Lasner, Hendry, Kretzsch, Priboroski). The likeliest reason is a plain one: the book records who held squadron command near the end of the war, while Allen's list runs back through the men he served under earlier in his tour.

The 3rd Bomb Group — “The Grim Reapers”

The 3rd Bombardment Group deployed to Australia in early 1942 and flew under the newly formed Fifth Air Force out of Port Moresby by January 1943, with five squadrons: HQ, 8th, 13th, 89th, and 90th. In late 1942, engineering officer Maj. Paul “Pappy” Gunn modified the group's B-25s at Eagle Farm, Brisbane — stripping the glass bombardier nose and packing it with four fixed .50-cal guns, plus twin “package guns” on each side, cleared to attack at masthead height. The entire 90th Squadron was flying these “commerce destroyer” strafers by February 1943. At the Battle of the Bismarck Sea (2–3 March 1943) — the first sea-level B-25 strafing attack of the war — the 90th hit every ship in an 18-vessel Japanese convoy. The group struck Wewak's airfields on 17 August 1943, earning a Distinguished Unit Citation, then staged from Dobodura and Nadzab as the front moved forward through 1943–44.

Sources: Army Air Corps Museum; OzAtWar field-modification records.

The 5th Air Force — The Command Above His Own

The 3rd Bomb Group flew under the Fifth Air Force, activated in Australia on 3 September 1942 under Maj. Gen. George C. Kenney, headquartered in Brisbane. Kenney had landed in Brisbane just weeks earlier, on 29 July 1942, made a rapid tour of the Fifth's bases across northern Queensland, and fired its entire command air staff. At its founding the Fifth had five bomber groups on hand — the 3rd (Allen's own, flying B-25s, A-20s, and A-24s), the 19th, 22nd, 38th, and 43rd — under roughly 1,600 officers and 18,000 men servicewide. Brig. Gen. Kenneth Walker commanded the Fifth's Bomber Command from Townsville; the Fifth opened sustained operations against the Japanese in Papua New Guinea on 4 November 1942. In June 1944 it combined with the Thirteenth Air Force to form the Far East Air Forces, still under Kenney, with Lt. Gen. Ennis Whitehead taking direct command of the Fifth.

Source: OzAtWar (Peter Dunn, ozatwar.com).

The Group's Medal of Honor

The 3rd Bomb Group's own history book records one Medal of Honor, earned in the group's costliest raid: the low-level strike on Simpson Harbor, Rabaul, November 2, 1943, that sank 104,000 tons of Japanese shipping but flew the Mitchells directly into the harbor's ring of naval anti-aircraft fire. Major Raymond H. Wilkins, his own bomber already damaged, deliberately flew it into the heaviest fire from two Japanese heavy cruisers — drawing their guns onto himself and away from the rest of his squadron so they could get through. He did not survive the mission. The book's own words: “He sacrificed his life to save the aircraft of his squadron.” Nobody wrote down which of the group's squadrons Wilkins flew with, so this belongs here as the wider group's story rather than as Allen's own — there is nothing to say the 90th was on that particular raid.

Source: The Reaper's Harvest, 3rd Attack Group Association.

The Planes He Photographed

One of Them Names Itself: 41-12962

The Mitchell photographed low over the water from another aircraft carries 112962 in white on the outer face of the near fin — the tail number for serial 41-12962. That is enough to look up, and it comes back.

Pacific Wrecks records 41-12962 as a B-25C of the Fifth Air Force, 3rd Bombardment Group, 13th Bombardment Squadron, carrying the names Calamity Jane and M & M. So this is Allen’s own group and not his own squadron: the 13th, not the 90th. It is the only aircraft in the collection identified from a serial rather than from a name painted on the nose.

The number is also close to home in another sense. Spook II was 41-12969. Seven airframes apart in the same contract block, the two of them were built and delivered within days of each other and ended up in the same group.

The frame dates itself as well. The star on the rear fuselage is a plain disc with no side bars, the marking carried until the end of June 1943, so the photograph was made before then.

Sources: the print; Pacific Wrecks — B-25C “Calamity Jane / M & M”, serial 41-12962.

A P-40 with White Crosses, and Where It Was Taken likely

The fighter with the crosses is a Curtiss P-40: a liquid-cooled inline engine under a long nose, a low wing and a tailwheel. It is not a P-39, which sits on a nose wheel.

Everything about the setting says the United States rather than the Pacific. Pine woods along the far edge of the field, a long open-sided shed, a boom truck, grass rather than pierced steel plank. The back of the print is rubber-stamped “DO NOT MAIL” and carries no censor mark, which is a stateside handling stamp and not a theatre one.

Large white crosses on the fuselage and upper wing, with the fin and rudder in broad stripes, are exercise markings — put on so that umpires and the other side can tell friend from enemy in a war game. Allen was at the Carolina Manoeuvres of 16–28 November 1941; he wrote them on the back of one of his own photographs. Pine woods, a grass field and aircraft painted up for an exercise fit that, and this document goes no further than saying so. Nothing in the frame carries a date, a unit or a place.

Sources: the print and its reverse; Allen’s own annotation on a Carolina Manoeuvres photograph.

The Frame That Was Not “Fat Cat II”

One caption in this chapter has been calling an aeroplane Fat Cat II. Looked at closely, the print does not say so. What is actually on the fuselage is a Roman II in white serif capitals, with a bar above it and a bar below, and shark-mouth teeth in the corner of the frame. The word in front of the numeral is not in the picture at all: the top edge of the print cuts it off.

Two things argue against Fat Cat and for Spook II. The photograph of Spook II reproduced in the group’s own printed history shows the II drawn with a bar above and below — the same numeral, drawn the same way. And the frames in this collection that do carry Fat Cat’s name carry a cat with it, in a bow tie, in a deck chair or an armchair, with a cigar or a cigarette; none of them carries a numeral. No Fat Cat II turns up anywhere else here, or in the group history.

And then the two prints settle it between them. Set side by side they are one aeroplane: the same shark mouth at the nose, the same cluster of package gun barrels aft, the same mainwheel with its white hubcap, the same scrub behind the tail, and the same II with its bars in the same place on the fuselage. In the other frame the word SPOOK stands above that numeral in large white capitals. In this one the top edge of the paper cuts it off. Three men stand in front of her in each, and they are not the same three.

So this is another frame of Spook II. Allen’s own frames of her come to four, three of them separate photographs.

Sources: the print; The Reaper’s Harvest, 3rd Bomb Group.

Every Aeroplane in the Collection, With a Name

Asked whether the aeroplanes had all been found, the honest first answer was that nobody had counted. So all two hundred and fifty-three photographs in the wartime chapters were opened and looked at, one at a time, with two questions: is there an aeroplane in this, and is there anything painted on it. Fifty-five have an aeroplane somewhere in the frame. Thirty-seven carry a name, a number, a stencil or a piece of nose art.

Eleven aircraft are named on prints Allen made himself: Hell’s Fire, with a Japanese flag painted on her nose and about twenty-five mission symbols under the cockpit; Chatter Box; Shy-Baby; Spook II; Fat Cat; Early Bird, under her white rooster; Merchant; Algernon IV, with a mission tally of her own; Sad Sack; and the one whose sun-bleached name reads CHIC…A-HIYA. Five more are named on the one page he kept out of the group’s printed history: The Skipper, Mark’s Hope, Old Thundermug, Darlin Dot and The Buckeye Kid, each nose carrying the 90th’s two-dice-in-a-disc.

Then there are the ones that got away. A shark-mouthed nose whose name ends in a long-tailed K, the rest above the top edge of the print. A fuselage with a pin-up of a woman seated on a bomb and no lettering anywhere near her. A propeller blade whose stencil gives a drawing number, an Air Corps number and the line INSTALLED-7-10-42. And a Mitchell low over the water with 41-1290[2] on her fin, four aircraft away in the serial block from Spook II.

Counting a squadron’s aircraft from one man’s snapshots is not the same as counting the squadron’s aircraft, and this list does not pretend to be an inventory of the 90th. It is what he pointed a camera at.

Method: all 253 wartime photographs read at full size, September 2026.

Fat Cat, and Why She Photographs Light

Every other aeroplane in this collection photographs dark. Fat Cat is pale, and there is a reason for it that the group wrote down at the time. She had flown seventy-three missions and two hundred and forty-five combat hours when she cracked up on landing on 22 June. Rather than scrap her the group stripped two thousand two hundred pounds of equipment out of her, refinished her in bright silver, and put her on the milk run down to the Australian mainland to bring back fresh food and drink for the men.

So the light finish in this frame is not bare factory metal and not a trick of exposure. It is a combat aeroplane that had been retired into the job of fetching the squadrons their supper. The nose art here — the cat in its deck chair, the cigarette, the parasol beneath, the name staggered inside quotation marks — matches detail for detail the photographs of Fat Cat made at Dobodura in about September 1943 by Jack Heyn, who had crossed the Pacific on the same ship as Allen. Allen was at Dobodura from 21 May 1943 and sailed for home on 29 September 1944, which brackets this picture.

Two things are deliberately not said. This was the group’s aeroplane by then, rebuilt by men of four squadrons, so she is not written here as the 90th’s. And no serial is printed in the caption: Pacific Wrecks records Fat Cat as B-25C 41-12449, but no contemporary source seen here carries a serial at all, and the wartime article that names her gives none. There was also more than one. Elsewhere in this same collection is a quite different machine — olive drab, shark-mouthed, package guns, and a cat that is a head in a bow tie between the two words of her name rather than a cat in a chair. Two aeroplanes, two paintings, one name. A third print, which used to be filed here as “Fat Cat II” on the strength of a Roman numeral, has turned out to be Spook II with her name cut off by the edge of the paper, and has been moved out of Fat Cat’s file.

Sources: the 3rd Bomb Group’s own wartime paper, as quoted by Australia @ War; photographs of Fat Cat at Dobodura credited to Jack Heyn and Howard E. Poit; 3rd Attack Group Association; Pacific Wrecks (for the serial, reported and not confirmed).

Serial numbers and fates for the named B-25s in Allen's own collection, checked against the 3rd Bomb Group's surviving loss records and against its own official unit history, The Reaper's Harvest — the same book Allen kept, digitized in full by the 3rd Attack Group Association.

Spook II, 41-12969, was Larner's own aircraft. Leading his squadron into the attack at the Battle of the Bismarck Sea, March 3, 1943, he radioed his wingmen off his tail — “Get off my wing, you guys, get your own damn ships! This one's mine!” — and bore down alone on the largest ship in the convoy, a Teratsuki-class destroyer. The 3rd Bomb Group's strafers helped sink or damage every vessel in an 18-ship convoy that day, the action that proved low-level strafing tactics could work. Spook II — also logged as Onkle Rafe — stayed Larner's aircraft until she crashed on April 30, 1943.

Fat Cat, 41-12449, was flown by 1st Lt. William R. “Red” Johnson. Feather Merchant (also called Irene), 41-12442 — likely the “Merchant” in Allen's own photo — survived the war and was later put on display at Aitape High School. Chatter Box is harder to pin down: one detailed account gives 41-13088, piloted by Capt. Bob Chatt at the Bismarck Sea; the 3rd Bomb Group's own loss roster instead lists a “El Diablo III / Chatter Box,” 41-30088, pilot Howe, force-landed April 16, 1943 — possibly the same aircraft renamed, possibly two planes that shared a name, as squadrons sometimes did once one was lost. Hell's Fire, Early Bird, Shy-Baby, Algernon IV and Sad Sack have so far given up no serial numbers at all, neither in the unit's own history book nor in the outside aviation archives; a data plate or a nose number in a sharper print would settle them.

Sources: Pacific Wrecks, 3rd Bombardment Group aircraft roster; worldwarphotos.info; Warbird Information Exchange forum; The Reaper's Harvest (3rd Attack Group Association).

Chatter Box, In the Flesh

This print comes from the unit history book rather than from Allen's camera, and it puts a face to the aircraft named on the previous page: the 90th Squadron's own Chatter Box, shark's teeth painted across her nose, with the men of the squadron posed in front of her. The book's own caption reads simply, “90th Squadron men who participated in Bismarck Sea Battle.” Allen is not picked out anywhere in the crowd, and nothing in the frame says which of the two logged serial numbers this particular airframe wore. What it does give is the name, the nose art, and the squadron's own judgement that this was a photograph worth printing.

The Dutch B-25s

Allen’s memory here is exact, and the paperwork backs him. On 30 June 1941 the Netherlands Purchasing Commission contracted for 162 B-25C Mitchells, and the first eighteen began reaching Archerfield aerodrome outside Brisbane from 2 March 1942 — by which date the Netherlands East Indies they had been bought to defend was a week from surrender, Java capitulating on 8 March. The first dozen wore Netherlands Indies serials in the N5- series, N5-120 to N5-131, and the orange triangle national marking, and with no Dutch crews available to fly them a delivery agreement passed the bulk of the shipment to American units: of fifty-four aircraft, eighteen were to go to the Netherlands East Indies and thirty-six, plus six already landed, to the USAAF.

On 1 April 1942 a party from the 3rd Bomb Group flew down to Archerfield to collect them, and some went on flying for a time still carrying their Dutch serials. The Queensland Heritage Register entry for Charters Towers airfield states the same thing plainly: the group’s A-24 dive bombers were followed by B-25s intended for the Dutch in the East Indies and taken over by the Americans. The story is often told as a theft — Pappy Gunn helping himself to idle Dutch bombers — but a Dutch curator and the delivery agreement both point to a formal handover, and Allen’s own word, consigned, is closer to the documents than the legend is. The Dutch-language instruction pamphlets are his own contribution to the record: no published account we have found so much as mentions them, though they are exactly what a factory-consigned aeroplane would have arrived with.

Sources: IPMS Nederland — ML-KNIL B-25 Mitchell (English); Queensland Heritage Register 602739 — Charters Towers Airfield Bore Sight Range and Compass Swinging Platform; Australia @ War — How the 3rd Bomb Group obtained their B-25 Mitchells from the Dutch; Australia @ War — 3rd Bombardment Group.

Turning a bomber into a strafer

What Allen is describing from the inside is one of the most consequential field modifications of the Pacific war. The Mitchell came out of the factory as a medium bomber that attacked from altitude; the 3rd Bomb Group took it apart and rebuilt it to attack from treetop height. The bombsight and the bombardier went, because at fifty feet the pilot simply aims the aeroplane and lets the bombs go himself.

The retractable belly turret went too — useless at that height, and an expensive drag and weight penalty. In their place went four fixed .50 calibre Browning guns in the nose, fired through the old bomb-aiming panel, and four more in pairs in blister packages on the fuselage sides below the cockpit: eight forward-firing guns, all of them under the pilot’s thumb. The work began at Eagle Farm aerodrome in Brisbane about November and December 1942, with a dozen aircraft finished by February 1943, after which the programme moved to the air depot at Townsville, which had turned out roughly 175 converted B-25C and D strafers by September 1943. One small correction to offer gently: Allen remembers the extra guns as being out on the wings, while every technical account puts them in those fuselage-side packages — outboard of the crew, but not on the wing.

Sources: Australia @ War — B-25 Strafer Field Modifications; Aircraft Information — B-25C/D Strafers; Queensland Heritage Register 602739 — Charters Towers Airfield Bore Sight Range; Pacific Wrecks — 3rd Bombardment Group, North American B-25 Mitchells.

Pappy Gunn and the man from North American

The rebuild Allen lived through was the work of two men, one of whom was standing on his own airfield. Jack Fox was North American Aviation’s field service representative based at Charters Towers, and he worked up drawings showing .50 calibre guns built into the bombardier’s station and sent them back to North American’s California plant — drawings developed with Major Paul Irvin Gunn of the 3rd Bomb Group, universally known as Pappy. Gunn was an unlikely officer: born 18 October 1899, an enlisted Navy pilot who retired as a chief petty officer in December 1939 after twenty-one years, an instructor at Pensacola, and a founder’s partner in Philippine Air Lines.

When the Philippines fell his wife and four children were interned at Santo Tomas, which is generally held to explain a good deal about how hard he fought the rest of the war. Already a captain by the Java campaign, he had been commissioned straight into the Army Air Forces, and General Kenney later took him onto his personal staff as his special-projects man; North American eventually built versions of his armament ideas into production Mitchells. Gunn was killed when his aircraft went down in a storm over the Philippines on 11 October 1957.

Sources: Australia @ War — Paul Irvin “Pappy” Gunn in Australia during WW2; Wikipedia — Pappy Gunn; Australia @ War — B-25 Strafer Field Modifications; Aircraft Information — B-25C/D Strafers (Jack Fox and Major Paul I. Gunn, 3rd Bombardment Group).

The seat that was deleted

This is the quietest sentence in the interview and one of the most personal. A B-25 came to the 90th Squadron with a crew of six — two pilots, a bombardier, two gunners and a radio man. The strafer flew with three, and the trade Allen had taught himself by mail order from Cressona, and then studied at Scott Field from about June to October 1941 by his own reckoning, no longer had a seat in the aeroplane. Published crew tables for Fifth Air Force strafers do not agree with one another — some units kept a radio-gunner, and the factory-built B-25G and H still carried a radio operator — so what Allen is describing is what his own squadron actually flew, not a rule laid down for the whole air force.

What did not disappear was the work. Elsewhere in the same interview he lays out exactly what the section did instead: service the radio sets in the aircraft, run a ground radio station, and lay and maintain the telephone lines between every operations building and the tents of the men in charge — patching calls by hand on an antique switchboard whose little flaps dropped down when a call came in. He went home a Master Sergeant and a communications chief. The seat was deleted; the trade was not.

Sources: Aircraft Information — B-25C/D Strafers; Australia @ War — B-25 Strafer Field Modifications; Allen E. Sweigert interview transcript, part 1 (family recording); Allen E. Sweigert handwritten service record book, transcribed in the interview PDF (90th Bomb Squadron, 4-10-41 to 9-10-44).

The bomb on a parachute likely

Low-level attack only works if the bomb you drop does not catch you, and the answer was a small fragmentation bomb hung beneath a parachute. The canopy killed the bomb’s forward speed and an instantaneous fuze burst it in the open, so the aircraft that had dropped it was already clear of the blast. General Kenney is the man usually credited with pushing the parafrag into the South West Pacific, out of prewar Army experiments, and with finding a stock of them in war reserve before he left for Australia — though we have not yet found a published source we would stand behind for the year he began that work, the number shipped, or the fragment count so often quoted.

The use of them is beyond argument: on 12 October 1943 the 3rd Bomb Group went into Rapopo airfield with 20lb parafrags and .50 calibre fire, and from then on that pairing, delivered at treetop height, was the Grim Reapers’ signature. The group is also widely said to have flown the first parafrag mission of the war, over Buna airfield in Papua in September 1942, and one of the bombs is said to be displayed in the Second World War gallery of the National Museum of the United States Air Force; both are worth chasing down to a document, and the group’s own history, The Reaper’s Harvest, is the place to start. Even the nominal weight is unsettled: the sources give it as both 20 and 23 pounds.

Sources: National Museum of the United States Air Force — WWII Weapons (23-pound parafrag bomb on display); Wikipedia — 3rd Operations Group (lineage and WWII tactical innovations); History of War — 3rd Bombardment Group (parafrag use, Rapopo, October 1943); Wikipedia — George Kenney (no mention of parafrag development; instantaneous fuzes and the Pruth demonstration).

Four squadrons, and an older name

The transcript loses the last of the four numbers Allen is reciting, and it happens to be his own: the 8th, the 13th, the 89th and the 90th Bombardment Squadrons. The group they belonged to had one of the longest pedigrees in American military aviation. It was organised on 1 July 1919 as the Army Surveillance Group, became the 1st Surveillance Group that August, the 3rd Group (Attack) on 15 September 1921 and the 3rd Attack Group on 25 January 1923; it was redesignated 3rd Bombardment Group (Light) on 15 September 1939, briefly (Dive) from 28 September 1942, and (Light) again from 25 May 1943.

Officially a bombardment group for the whole of Allen’s service, it went on calling itself the 3rd Attack Group anyway — there is a hand-painted “3rd Attack Gp” sign in his own photographs — which, given what it was actually doing over New Guinea, was the more honest description. Its wartime station list reads like a fair copy of Allen’s own itinerary: Brisbane from 25 February 1942, Charters Towers from 10 March 1942, Port Moresby from 28 January 1943, Dobodura from 20 May 1943, Nadzab from 3 February 1944 and Hollandia from 12 May 1944. It came out of the war with a Distinguished Unit Citation for Papua and another for the Wewak strike of 17 August 1943 — the five citations often quoted for the group include its three Korean War awards — and a name nobody had to be told twice: the Grim Reapers.

Sources: Army Air Corps Museum — 3rd Bombardment Group (lineage, stations with dates, campaign credits); Wikipedia — 3rd Operations Group (full designation history); Pacific Wrecks — 3rd Bombardment Group “The Grim Reapers”; History of War — 3rd Bombardment Group (Wewak DUC, 17 August 1943; squadron list).

Aiming an aeroplane

Eight guns bolted rigidly to an airframe are aimed by aiming the aeroplane, which means that before a strafer is any use to anybody, somebody has to line its guns up on a measured target on the ground. At Charters Towers the concrete for doing exactly that is still there.

In mid-February 1943 the US Army put a requisition to the Queensland Department of Public Works for a bore sight range: a firing platform with an adjustable steel plate to take the nose wheel of tricycle-undercarriage aircraft such as the B-25, sighting some 1,180 feet down to a target area, together with a compass swinging platform. It is the only known example of its type in Queensland and was entered on the Queensland Heritage Register on 14 May 2010. One thing to be honest about: it went up after Allen’s group had gone north to New Guinea, and it served the fighter and bomber replacement training centre that took over the field. But it was built for his aeroplane, to solve the problem his squadron’s conversion had created.

Sources: Queensland Heritage Register 602739 — Charters Towers Airfield Bore Sight Range and Compass Swinging Platform; Wikipedia — Bore Sight Range and Compass Swinging Platform at Charters Towers Airfield; Queensland WWII Historic Places — Charters Towers Airfield.

Spook II, Four Photographs

Four of Allen’s photographs show the same aeroplane, and until his grandson said so none of them was recognised for what it was. Said out loud it is plain enough — SP is the front of her name and OK is the back of it, and a man walking round one aircraft on one afternoon catches it from both ends. She is Spook II, and a fifth frame gives the whole name at once: SPOOK in solid white capitals with a Roman II below it, drawn with a bar above and below.

One detail is worth recording and not over-reading. In the frame that shows the front of the name, the letters are outlined with a drop shadow; in the frames that show the back of it, they are solid white. That could be the nose lettered differently from the fuselage side, or one side of the aircraft differently from the other, or a repaint between one photograph and the next. This document briefly built a second aeroplane on that difference, reading the airframe in the SP frame as an A-20 Havoc rather than a Mitchell. Enlarged, that frame shows a long fuselage and a small dorsal dome and no tail at all, so there was nothing in it to support the reading, and it has been withdrawn.

Larner really did have two aircraft and really did carry the name from one to the next — an A-20A Havoc, serial 40-109, and then the B-25C, 41-12969 — and that is set out on its own elsewhere in this chapter. What is not claimed any more is that Allen photographed the first one.

The count is smaller than it looks. The SP frame appears twice in this document, once as a scan of the print and once as a phone photograph of the same print made to capture its back; so does the turret-loading frame. Six figures, four photographs, one aeroplane.

And the date holds. Spook II went into the ground at Dobodura on 30 April 1943 with all eight aboard, so every frame of her here was made before that, which makes them the earliest firmly dated aircraft pictures in the collection. The pencil on the back of one of them reads “Maj. Edward L…” — on a print of Larner’s own aeroplane, which is as close as this collection comes to naming the man in the cockpit.

Identification: Jeremy Sweigert, September 2026. Pacific Wrecks — B-25C Spook II / Onkle Rafe, serial 41-12969.

Margaret: the airplane in the photograph

The aircraft was B-25C Mitchell serial 41-12946, constructor’s number 82–5581, built by North American Aviation at Inglewood, California, and ferried overseas by way of Hickam Field and across the Pacific to Australia. Peter Dunn’s Australia @ War records it as the first strafer-modified B-25, arriving at Charters Towers on 8 December 1942 and assigned there to the 90th Bombardment Squadron, 3rd Bombardment Group — Allen’s own squadron, on Allen’s own field, three months after he turned twenty-four.

The conversion fitted eight forward-firing Browning .50 caliber machine guns: four grouped in the nose, set through the original bomb-aiming panel, and four more in blister packages hung two to a side on the forward fuselage. The Army Air Forces official history independently supports the timing, noting that the experimental model had passed its tests in December 1942 and that by April some thirty B-25Cs had been modified the same way. Allen described the same surgery from the inside of it, in his own words, without ever naming the airplane that proved it.

Sources: B-25 Strafer Field Modifications — Australia @ War (Peter Dunn); Pacific Wrecks — B-25C “Margaret” Serial Number 41-12946; The Army Air Forces in World War II, Vol. IV, ch. 5 (Craven & Cate, HyperWar); 3rd Bombardment Group — Australia @ War (Peter Dunn).

Who cut the guns in, and where the record goes soft likely

The idea belonged to Major Paul I. “Pappy” Gunn of the 3rd Bombardment Group — the old naval aviator Kenney had put in charge of special projects — working with Jack Fox, North American Aviation’s field service representative in Australia, who was based at Charters Towers alongside Allen’s squadron; Kenney gave the program his authorization after seeing what Gunn had already done to the A-20. Where the work on Margaret was actually done is less settled than the conversion itself.

The only published caption anyone has found for the aircraft places her in a hangar at Garbutt airfield at Townsville, in Base Depot Number 4; yet the same page’s account of the program says the modifications began at Eagle Farm in Brisbane about November or December 1942, and the big Townsville depot job it describes in detail is a later one — more than sixty Mitchells of the 345th Group, at Depot Number 2 at Mount Louisa, in August 1943. Which shed and whose hands finished Margaret is simply not on the record. Allen remembered the guns as being out on the wings, which is the one detail the written record does not support — the side packages hung in blisters on the fuselage under the cockpit, not on the wing. That is a sixty-year-old memory of a shape glimpsed from the radio compartment, not an error worth correcting so much as a reminder of how he saw the airplane.

Sources: B-25 Strafer Field Modifications — Australia @ War (Peter Dunn); Paul Irvin “Pappy” Gunn in Australia during WW2 — Australia @ War; B-25C/D Strafers — aircraftinformation.info; The Genesis of the B-25 Mitchell Strafer-Bomber — Tails Through Time.

“First conversion” and “first combat” are two different claims likely

Two aircraft carry a claim to being the first B-25 strafer, and both deserve their hearing here. Australia @ War names 41-12946 Margaret as the first strafer-modified B-25; a separate and equally common account names B-25C 41-12437, the airframe chosen for the initial tests and remembered ever since as “Pappy’s Folly.” That second aircraft has a particular resonance here, because Pacific Wrecks records it as one of the Mitchells built against the Netherlands East Indies order — delivered in February 1942 as N5-132, ferried to Australia, flown by the Dutch-manned No. 18 Squadron out of RAAF Fairbairn before the Army Air Forces took it over on 1 September 1942, and posted during 1943 to the 90th itself.

It was one of the Dutch-consigned machines Allen remembered arriving with Dutch markings and Dutch manuals. The combat claim is also narrower than it sounds: the Army Air Forces official history records that the 90th’s crews practiced for weeks on an old wreck lying off Port Moresby, and that the planes “went out twice during February against real targets, but each time failed to locate the reported vessels.” The Bismarck Sea was therefore the first time the strafers proved themselves in action, not the first time they went out looking for a fight — and nothing anyone wrote down will carry the claim further than that.

Sources: Pacific Wrecks — B-25C 41-12437 (“General Custer” / “Pappy’s Folly”); B-25C/D Strafers — aircraftinformation.info; B-25 Strafer Field Modifications — Australia @ War (Peter Dunn); The Army Air Forces in World War II, Vol. IV, ch. 5 (Craven & Cate, HyperWar).

Bismarck Sea, 3 March 1943: thirty-seven bombs, seventeen hits

The Army Air Forces official history records that twelve of the 90th Squadron’s B-25C-1s flew in the coordinated morning attack of 3 March 1943 — “probably the most successful attack of all” — and that out of the thirty-seven bombs dropped, seventeen were claimed as direct hits. The official history does not name the man who led them; for that the source is Kenney himself, quoted in Air & Space Forces Magazine: “Ed Larner with twelve of my new B-25 commerce destroyers skip-bombed,” and “Ed Larner’s squadron dropped thirty-seven 500-pound bombs, scoring seventeen direct hits.” Pacific Wrecks credits eight 90th Squadron aircraft with sinking a destroyer and two transports that day.

Across the battle of 2 to 4 March, Allied air power sank all eight Japanese transports and four of the eight escorting destroyers, and the low-level gunship Mitchell was never questioned again. Larner did not long outlive the vindication: he was killed over his own field in the spring of 1943, a loss the group set down in its own published history and nowhere online. Whether Margaret herself was one of the twelve in the air that morning, nobody wrote down — and it would be wrong to put her among them.

Sources: The Army Air Forces in World War II, Vol. IV, ch. 5 (Craven & Cate, HyperWar); Pacific Wrecks — Battle of the Bismarck Sea; Pacific Wrecks — 90th Bombardment Squadron (90th BS); Victory in the Bismarck Sea — Air & Space Forces Magazine.

What became of her, and the other photograph

Margaret did not die in combat. On 7 August 1943 she took off from Port Moresby under 1st Lt Joseph H. Castelli on a mission to practice formation flying, flew too low, and struck the sea about two miles due east of Buna airfield in Papua; Herschel Piper’s diary describes the left engine catching the water, the propeller breaking away, the right wing digging in, and the airplane going over on its back and sinking in a matter of seconds. All four men aboard were killed: 1st Lt Castelli, 2nd Lt Hugh A. Phelps, Sgt Daniel W. Atkins and Pfc Harry J. Spies.

Only Atkins was recovered, and he was buried afterward at Woodlawn Cemetery in Spray, North Carolina; the other three are named on the tablets of the missing at Manila American Cemetery. The aircraft was condemned two days later, and no Missing Air Crew Report was raised. The only published photograph of her located anywhere is a single Australia @ War image, captioned as showing her in a hangar at Garbutt airfield, Base Depot Number 4; Allen’s photograph, taken out on the line with four of his squadron standing beneath the nose and a shark mouth painted under her name, appears to be a second and previously unpublished view of the same aircraft. The shark mouth is consistent with 90th Squadron practice, which gave the same marking to the Mitchells Mortimer (41-12443) and Runts Roost (41-29727).

How These Prints Are Known to Be His

Two things run through the whole collection and neither is a stamp. The first is the pencil. The writing on the backs — the names, the places, the months, “outside hut in New Guinea. Nov. 1942”, “Driving… in Australia” — is Allen’s own hand, on print after print. A man does not caption another man’s photographs in his own pencil and then keep them for sixty-six years.

The second is that he is in a good many of them, and his grandson is still going through and saying which. That does not make them less his. It means that on those afternoons somebody else pressed the button — a friend borrowing the camera, most likely his — and the negative came home in his footlocker all the same. This document does not keep a running tally of which frames he stands in; where he is identified, the caption says so and says who identified him.

“Greetings — New Guinea”, and a Man Called Joe

The village house on the sand carries a message across its foreground in thin white cursive: “Greetings — New Guinea”, and then, below and to the right where a signature goes, “Joe”.

How it was made is worth a sentence. Letters drawn in sand with a finger come out broad and ragged, and the groove takes a shadow, so it prints dark on one side. These are thin, even and confident, they print bright white, and the sand grain runs on underneath them undisturbed. That is what a needle in the emulsion of a negative looks like: scratch the film and the print comes back with a clean white line. Somebody made this print to send, and signed the sending.

Who Joe was, this document cannot say. It is not Allen. He may be one of the men whose names are pencilled on the backs of the prints in the same batch, or the greeting may be addressed to a Joe at home rather than signed by one. It is the only photograph in the collection that has been written on rather than written behind.

Source: the print itself.

Where These Were Taken, and What They Are Of

Two different questions get confused here, so this note separates them before it counts anything.

Where they were taken. Most of this collection was made in New Guinea. From the squadron’s move up to Port Moresby at the end of January 1943 until he sailed for home on 29 September 1944 — twenty months — the island is the ground under nearly every frame in this chapter. The camps, the strips, the revetments, the mess queue, the aeroplanes, the men: that is New Guinea in the picture, whatever the picture is of.

What they are of. Here the count has moved, twice, and in opposite directions. A sweep of the two hundred and fourteen photographs then in this document found exactly two that contain New Guinean people. Then, on 25 September 2026, seventeen more prints arrived that had never been handed over — and six of them are New Guinea and the people who lived there. A group of thirty or more gathered on burnt-over ground; three young women in flower garlands and fibre skirts; a gathering in long fibre cloaks; two children carrying loads on their heads; two men at the edge of a crowd, one of them dressed as a village constable; and a mixed group of servicemen and New Guineans beside the trucks.

So the figure is eight, not two, and the earlier version of this note said he “brought back almost nothing of it.” That was wrong, and it was wrong because of what had been given to the people writing this document rather than because of what he photographed. The lesson is about the archive and not about him.

What still holds is the proportion. Eight frames of the island’s people, and four more with the country itself as the subject, out of well over two hundred. He was a radio man on an airstrip and what he photographed was mostly what he lived among. He told his grandson “we made several trips up to the native village to see how it was” — the sentence at the head of this section — and now there are pictures of those trips.

The sweep also took something away, and that stands. The group photograph on the slope had been captioned here as showing four Papuans. Enlarged, two of the four are American servicemen in the hard brim shadow of their sun helmets, one with a clear tan line at the collar. A man at the right of a Japanese prisoner column was flagged and refused: his hair is smooth and swept back with a highlight across the crown, which tightly curled hair does not do.

Method: every frame read at full size; all flags adversarially reviewed, September 2026.

Three Names, and a Date Two Months Early

This caption used to say that only one word on the back could be read. Enlarged and sharpened, the pencil gives up the lot: three surnames one under another — Dutchman, Gillmer, Haskin — and beneath them “outside hut in New Guinea. Nov. 1942.” Three names for three men on a rail, and the photograph placed and dated by the man who wrote on it.

Two of the three were already in this chapter, on other prints in the same batch. A signature read as Haskin is on the back of a photograph marked “N. G.” for New Guinea, and James W. Gillmer of Miami, Florida, is named on a portrait. The third name is new. Dutchman appears nowhere else here — unless it is the clipped line on the back of another portrait in this batch, the one that gives a home town of Westphalia, Kansas. That is a suggestion and not an identification. The print is undamaged; it is the photograph of the print that cuts the name off, so another exposure of that back would settle it.

The date is the part that matters. The squadron’s own record has it arriving at Port Moresby on 28 January 1943. This back says New Guinea in November 1942, spelled out, two months earlier — and it is the second print in the collection to say so, after the one marked “N. G.” and 11/42. A single abbreviation could be misread. Two backs in two different hands, one of them writing the place out in full, are harder to argue with. Whatever the paper movement of the squadron was, men of it were on the island, and photographing each other outside a hut, by November 1942.

One question belongs to the family rather than to the record. “Dutchman” heads a list of three names on a picture of three men, in a collection kept by a Pennsylvania Dutchman from Schuylkill County. Whether it was what they called Allen, or what they called somebody else, nothing here can say.

The Bismarck Sea, and the Man in the Cockpit

On 3 March 1943 the 90th Squadron took its rebuilt Mitchells against a Japanese convoy, and settled the argument about whether the strafer worked.

The man in the cockpit was Allen’s own squadron commander

The photograph reproduced here from the group history book is not a picture of a stranger. Edward Lawrence Larner was born in San Francisco on 1 July 1917 and reported to the 3rd Bomb Group’s 89th Squadron on 28 October 1942, a transfer in from the 46th Bomb Group with more than eight hundred flying hours behind him; General Kenney noticed him within a fortnight and set the verdict down himself — “That lad was good. He had fire, leadership and guts.” By the turn of the year he was checking pilots out on the newly converted strafer B-25s, and he did that work in Queensland, not New Guinea: the 90th Bombardment Squadron was still at Charters Towers and did not move up to 3 Mile Drome at Port Moresby until 28 January 1943.

He was a major by the time he led that squadron into its first great action. The family’s own record places Allen in the 90th, and that is why this man belongs in Allen’s story: the officer in the cockpit was his squadron commander. Which photograph sits on the page he holds up is something the sources cannot settle for us.

Sources: Pilot Spotlight: Edward L. Larner Makes History — International Historical Research Associates; Edward Larner — Hall of Valor; Edward Lawrence Larner (1917–1943) — Find a Grave Memorial; Pacific Wrecks — Major Edward Larner in the cockpit of B-25C Spook II / Onkle Rafe 41-12969.

The convoy, and the morning of 3 March 1943

Eight Japanese transports and eight escorting destroyers sailed from Simpson Harbour at Rabaul on 28 February 1943 carrying roughly 6,900 troops for Lae, with about a hundred fighters assigned to cover them. On the morning of 3 March the Fifth Air Force and the RAAF struck the convoy in one stacked sequence: B-17s from about seven thousand feet to scatter it, B-25s releasing between three and six thousand, then thirteen Australian Beaufighters in at deck height to beat down the anti-aircraft guns, then Major Larner with twelve of the modified B-25s coming in at mast height, a squadron of A-20s behind them and P-38s overhead. By daybreak on 4 March all eight transports and four of the eight destroyers had been sunk.

The count of men is where the accounts part: the Australian War Memorial gives about 2,890 Japanese dead and only 850 ashore at Lae, while other histories carry at least three thousand dead and some twelve hundred landed. On one figure they agree — about twenty-seven hundred were pulled from the water and taken back to Rabaul. The 90th Squadron had itself reached Moresby only five weeks earlier, on 28 January 1943.

Sources: Battle of the Bismarck Sea, 2-4 March 1943 — Australian War Memorial; Victory in the Bismarck Sea — Air & Space Forces Magazine; March 2-4, 1943: Battle of the Bismarck Sea — Fold3 HQ; Battle of the Bismarck Sea — Wikipedia.

Why the battle is called the proof of the strafer

The whole of the 90th Squadron was in operation with strafer B-25s by the beginning of February 1943, each aircraft carrying eight forward-firing fifty-calibre guns — four in the nose, four in side packages — with two more in the top turret that could be swivelled forward, and the Bismarck Sea was the first time the converted aeroplanes were thrown at a convoy. Kenney’s own account credits Larner’s squadron with dropping thirty-seven five-hundred-pound bombs and claiming seventeen direct hits that morning, a claim and not a count, as the Australian War Memorial is careful to record it; unit records catalogued by Pacific Wrecks credit eight B-25s of the 90th with a destroyer and two transports.

The tactic worked because it was sequenced: the high-level bombing scattered the convoy and the Beaufighters silenced the guns, and only then did the mast-height aircraft go in. Every account reaches the same verdict: the idea had been proved, and decisively. What Allen describes in his own words is exactly that conversion, from a bombing outfit with a bombardier and a radio man to a low-level strafing outfit that no longer needed either.

Sources: B-25 Strafer Field Modifications — Australia @ War (Peter Dunn); Victory in the Bismarck Sea — Air & Space Forces Magazine; Pacific Wrecks — 90th Bombardment Squadron; Battle of the Bismarck Sea, 2-4 March 1943 — Australian War Memorial (records the 17 hits as a claim).

The claims were roughly double the result, and the record says so

A memorial should not repeat the wartime score sheet without saying what happened to it. The Fifth Air Force claim announced at the time ran to thirteen or fourteen merchant ships and seven destroyers sunk, and MacArthur’s communique went further still, at twenty-two ships; the figure that postwar accounting settled on, and that the Australian War Memorial and the standard modern histories carry, is eight transports and four destroyers. The over-claim was honest rather than dishonest, the predictable arithmetic of many aircraft attacking the same burning ships in a few minutes of smoke and spray. It does not reduce the battle at all, because the convoy was in fact annihilated and the reinforcement of Lae by sea was finished. It is simply the difference between what the men were told that week and what the ledger closed at.

Sources: Victory in the Bismarck Sea — Air & Space Forces Magazine; Battle of the Bismarck Sea, 2-4 March 1943 — Australian War Memorial; Battle of the Bismarck Sea — Wikipedia.

Spook and Spook II were two different aeroplanes

The name belonged to Larner and he carried it from one aircraft to the next. The first Spook was an A-20A Havoc, serial 40-109, on the 89th Squadron’s books, and Pacific Wrecks records that Larner named it after his wife’s nickname.

That aeroplane nosed over on takeoff from 3-Mile Drome at Port Moresby on 26 January 1943 under a different pilot, First Lieutenant Turner Williamson, who died in the wreckage; his gunner got out. The second was a B-25C, serial 41-12969, formerly on the 13th Squadron’s books and converted to a strafer shortly before the Bismarck Sea, and it carried two names on the nose, Spook II and Onkle Rafe. If the print in Allen’s collection shows a B-25 and not the slimmer A-20, then it is the second aircraft. What is still open is whether any of those frames is one that has been published. That is worth pinning down, because the copy catalogued by Pacific Wrecks is a cockpit portrait credited to the 3rd Bomb Group by way of Joseph Masiero, and it may not be the same photograph the family holds.

Sources: Pacific Wrecks — B-25C Spook II / Onkle Rafe Serial Number 41-12969; Pacific Wrecks — A-20A Spook Serial Number 40-109; Pacific Wrecks — Major Edward Larner in the cockpit of B-25C Spook II / Onkle Rafe 41-12969.

Larner’s decorations: what can be proved and what cannot likely

The award that can be documented is a posthumous Silver Star, recorded to Major Edward L. Larner, Air Corps, service number O-382601, 90th Bombardment Squadron, 3rd Bombardment Group (Light), Fifth Air Force, for gallantry as the pilot of a B-25 in the South West Pacific. The action dates on that record are given only as 1942 and 1943, so it cannot be tied to one mission. Some secondary accounts say that at the time of his death he had been recommended for the Distinguished Service Cross for the Bismarck Sea, but no source we can reach says so in its own words, and no roll of recipients has yet been searched properly for his name. The honest position, then: the Silver Star is documented, and the Distinguished Service Cross is a report we can neither confirm nor dismiss. A copy of his service record or a Fifth Air Force general order would settle it.

Sources: Edward Larner — Hall of Valor: Silver Star; Battle of the Bismarck Sea: A 15-Minute War in the South Pacific — Warfare History Network; Distinguished Service Cross Recipients, World War II, Army E-F — Home of Heroes.

How Larner died, and where the accounts disagree

He was killed at Dobodura on 30 April 1943, eight weeks after the Bismarck Sea, and nobody aboard Spook II survived. Eight men are named in the record: Larner, co-pilot Frederick H. Ensminger, navigator John J. Clarke, upper gunner Columbus G. Pelham, radio operator Vernon Moore, engineer Berj Manuelian, assistant engineer Robert L. Fecitt, and an Australian Army passenger, Captain Albert F. J. White.

The sources do not agree on how many were aboard — Pacific Wrecks says nine, and one published account says ten — so a ninth and perhaps a tenth man died unnamed here. The accident summary has the aircraft taking off from Dobodura, buzzing the fighter strip, pulling up to break away and falling off on one wing from about two hundred feet, with the left engine found to have cut and no height left to recover. A second account describes a heavily loaded aeroplane stalling at the top of the climbing turn he was known for, and has him landing rather than leaving. They agree on the date, the place, the aircraft and the fact that nobody walked away; they do not settle whether he was going out or coming in. He was twenty-five, and he is buried in San Francisco National Cemetery. Allen’s own squadron moved up to Dobodura three weeks after the crash.
