The mop crew was an official program likely

The stage crew Allen watched may well have been working from a kit. The Army’s Special Services Division ran a formal soldier-show program, issuing complete do-it-yourself packages that contained the script, orchestrations, set and costume designs, choreography and even program templates, so that men at a station could build the scenery, play the instruments and perform for each other. Four of these Blueprint Specials reached the field — About Face!, written by Frank Loesser, Arnold Auerbach and others; Hi, Yank!, built around Sgt. George Baker’s Sad Sack; P.F.C.

Mary Brown; and OK, USA! — and alongside them came the USO-Camp Shows “At Ease” volumes and the Army’s numbered Soldier Shows Folios, of which the surviving collection runs from No. 9 to No. 31, the latest of them dated 1945. A 1944 sketch-writing contest, the joint work of the Writers’ War Board and the Army’s Special Service, produced GI Prize Winning Blackouts, a published collection of short comic scenes about Army life chosen from hundreds submitted worldwide. A “blackout” is precisely the shape of what Allen saw: men, props, a known tune, a substituted lyric, and a fast curtain. No source names the show at Atlantic City, or puts that latrine lyric in any printed script — only the machinery that made such evenings routine.

Sources: Soldier Shows — U.S. Army “Blueprint Specials” and Other Soldier Shows, University of North Texas Libraries; Soldier Shows Collection — UNT Digital Library; The Comedy Propaganda Machine: The Soldier Sketch Writing Contest of World War II — University of Maryland.

Parody was the enlisted man’s own repertoire

The Army tried to give soldiers a songbook. The Army Song Book of 1941 offered sixty-seven patriotic, folk and service numbers and organized sing-alongs to go with them, and the men largely declined; their rule was spontaneous or nothing, and they snubbed the Army’s own song sessions.

What they sang instead were parodies, new words fitted to tunes everyone already carried, and the practice is documented in detail: “Bless ‘Em All” was the song American servicemen most liked to sing and to rewrite, and men set their own verses to “The Battle Hymn of the Republic,” “Down in the Valley,” “Mademoiselle from Armentieres” and “As Time Goes By.” Eric Posselt collected such material in Give Out! in 1943, and postal authorities called it lewd and obscene and banned it from the mail, while the FBI took an interest in “Gee, But I Want to Go Home” before concluding it was good-natured griping about Army food. Paul Fussell, writing later about his own war, described soldiers’ profanity as a licensed way for millions of conscripts to register bitterness and anger they could not otherwise speak; the parodies did the same work with a tune underneath. Allen’s latrine version of “Begin the Beguine” is a specimen of exactly that genre, and one of its mildest and most repeatable.

Sources: World War II Soldiers Loved to Sing — Provided They Got to Sing Their Way — HistoryNet; Blood on the Risers — Wikipedia; Soldiers’ Songs: The Folklore of the Powerless — Buffalo State College.

The song everyone in the room already knew

Cole Porter wrote “Begin the Beguine” on a 1935 cruise aboard the Franconia, between Kalabahi and Fiji, and gave it to the Broadway musical Jubilee, where June Knight introduced it at the Imperial Theatre in October 1935; the show failed, the song did not catch, and the musicologist Alec Wilder later called it “a maverick, it is an unprecedented experiment,” a tune he could not sing or whistle through without the printed music. What made it universal was Artie Shaw. Shaw recorded Jerry Gray’s arrangement at RCA Victor’s Studio 2 in New York on 24 July 1938; the men at RCA thought the idea “a complete waste of time” and put it on the B-side of “Indian Love Call,” the number everyone, Shaw included, expected to sell.

The disc jockeys chose the beguine side instead. It reached number three, made Shaw a national figure, and by the 1960s was reckoned to have sold some six and a half million copies. The Library of Congress essay on the record adds one detail this family will want: Porter’s tune, it says, was inspired by a folk number he had heard in New Guinea. That is the reason the gag Allen describes worked at all — by the autumn of 1944 a stage crew had only to walk out with mops and brooms and hit the first phrase, and every man in the hall supplied the original words in his head while the substituted ones landed on top of them.

Sources: Begin the Beguine — Wikipedia; “Begin the Beguine” — Artie Shaw & His Orchestra (1938), National Recording Preservation Board essay, Library of Congress; Artie Shaw — Wikipedia; Artie Shaw And His Orchestra, Begin The Beguine (1945 jukebox pressing) — Discogs.

“A 21-day delay en route” — and a hotel in Atlantic City

“Delay en route” is not soldier’s slang; it is an Army administrative term for authorised travel time granted between duty stations, governed for enlisted men by the Army’s regulations on furloughs, passes and delays. For Army Air Forces men back from overseas, that leave at home was followed by a redistribution station, and Allen’s was the first one the AAF ever opened. The AAF activated its Redistribution Center at Atlantic City on 15 August 1943, along with Redistribution Station No. 1 there and Station No. 2 at Miami Beach, with the mission “to receive all Army Air Forces Personnel returning to the continental United States from overseas” and then, after examination and re-evaluation, to assign them to appropriate stations, detail them to rest camps or effect their separation from the service.

The hotel he reported to was one of forty-seven Atlantic City hotels the Army had leased at a dollar a day per room; Chalfonte-Haddon Hall and the Traymore had together become Thomas M. England General Hospital, the Traymore holding its convalescent centre with its rehabilitation equipment for amputees. So his two interviews — the one where he named three stations near home, and the one where he was offered Atlantic City itself at the price of two grades — were the “examination and re-evaluation” the directive describes, and the dances, the shows and the mops-and-brooms latrine version of “Begin the Beguine” were part of the design rather than a windfall. At the Santa Monica station the Army set aside a whole hotel for married soldiers, and something like it at Atlantic City is the likeliest reason the honeymoon could simply carry on.

What two grades were worth likely

Under the Pay Readjustment Act of 1942, effective 1 June 1942, which set the rates for nearly the whole war, a first-grade noncommissioned officer, a master sergeant, drew $138.00 a month in base pay under three years of service and $144.90 with more than three years; two grades below him, in the third grade, a staff sergeant or technician third grade drew $96.00 and $100.80. For a man who had been in federal service since 16 September 1940, and so past the three-year mark, accepting the Atlantic City posting on those terms meant handing back $44.10 a month, a little over thirty percent of his base pay, along with the standing of the top enlisted grade he had reached overseas.

The structural reason such an offer could be made at all is the Table of Organization, the document that “established a unit’s strength by duty position and grade”: when every authorized slot at a grade was filled, no further man could be carried in it, and the making and breaking of noncommissioned officers ran on the Army’s own enlisted-grade regulations rather than on a commander’s goodwill. Allen’s account of DeRidder shows the same squeeze from the other side, a base authorized one master sergeant with five returned master sergeants standing in front of it and a major proposing a school and a competition for the single place. What the record does not settle is whether a formal Army Air Forces policy in 1944 and 1945 directed redistribution stations to seek such reductions — the official history of the redistribution program is silent on grade — or whether it was a local expedient; on that, his sentence is the evidence.

Sources: 1942–1946 Military Pay Chart (Pay Readjustment Act of 1942) — NavyCS; Comparison of military and civilian pay in WWII, Barron’s (reprinted); Assignment & Promotion — The American Soldier in World War II; United States Army enlisted rank insignia of World War II — Wikipedia.

Coffeyville, Kansas, was a real station

The town the tent used to laugh about had an Army airfield seven miles to its northeast, on a 1,456-acre tract where construction began on 1 June 1942; it was activated on 17 June 1942 as an Army Air Forces basic flying school, one of more than a dozen AAF fields Kansas would hold before the war ended. Basic pilot training in BT-13 Valiants opened on 14 November 1942 with Class 43-C, of whose 137 cadets 116 graduated on 13 January 1943, and in sixteen classes some 4,840 cadets and aviation students began the course while 3,881 finished it.

On 1 June 1944 the field passed to Third Air Force, which organized there the Coffeyville Replacement Training Unit (Photo Reconnaissance), redesignated three months later the Coffeyville Combat Crew Training Station (Photo Reconnaissance); the first pilots reported on 12 June 1944, and more than 460 finished their training in the twelve months that followed, first on the F-10, the camera version of the B-25, and then increasingly on the F-5, the unarmed camera version of the P-38. That is the station waiting behind the name on the Atlantic City bulletin board in the autumn of 1944, and its first camera ships were built on the same airframe Allen’s own squadron had flown across New Guinea. A War Department telegram of 2 October 1945 authorized announcing the field’s temporary inactivation, and the deed passed to the city on 21 July 1947; it is the Coffeyville Municipal Airport today.

Sources: Coffeyville Army Air Field — Wikipedia; Kansas World War II army airfields — Wikipedia; Coffeyville Municipal Airport — Wikipedia.

The 369th, and a move he missed by one day

The outfit Allen joined had a short and entirely domestic life. The 369th Fighter Group was constituted on 26 May 1943 and activated at Hamilton Field, California on 1 August 1943; it worked as a fighter Replacement Training Unit through Marysville and Oroville, came back to Hamilton, then moved to DeRidder Army Air Base on 28 March 1944, where it took part in air-ground manoeuvres and demonstrations including the Louisiana Manoeuvres of summer 1944.

It flew P-39 Airacobras, then A-36 Apaches, P-40 Warhawks and finally P-51 Mustangs, and it never went overseas; it was inactivated on 10 August 1945. On 31 January 1945 Stuttgart Army Air Field, Arkansas — built in 1942 under the 70,000 Pilot Training Program, first a glider school and then an advanced twin-engine flying school, with four 5,000-foot runways and seven auxiliary fields — was transferred to Third Air Force and became a replacement training school under the 369th. The group moved there on 8 February 1945, which is the move Allen missed by a single day, lying in the DeRidder hospital with malaria while his wife waited at the hotel in town.

Sources: 369th Fighter Group — Wikipedia; 369th Fighter Group — Army Air Corps Museum; 369th Fighter Group — History of War; Stuttgart Army Air Field (Arkansas) — Wikipedia.

DeRidder: one slot, five master sergeants likely

DeRidder Army Air Base had been built on land leased from the Beauregard Parish Police Jury from 1 July 1941, and by the wet night Allen arrived it was in its last phase: on 31 March 1944 the II Tactical Air Division of III Tactical Air Command was assigned there to conduct replacement training, and training at DeRidder ceased altogether in February 1945. We could not find any published source describing DeRidder as an “air raid warning school,” so that part of his account stands on his word alone — and the command’s own stated mission runs to dive bombardment, light bombardment and reconnaissance training, with no mention of aircraft-warning work, so the record neither backs him nor contradicts him here.

What the record does confirm is the door he talked his way through. The 369th Fighter Group, with its 398th, 399th and 400th Fighter Squadrons, had been stationed at DeRidder since 28 March 1944 — so a major picking up a telephone and finding three openings in a fighter squadron is entirely consistent with what was sitting on that same field. His own explanation of why he refused the competition is worth keeping as he said it: men who had done the work for years should not have to compete against someone given a crash course in it.

Sources: Beauregard Regional Airport (DeRidder Army Air Base) — Wikipedia; 369th Fighter Group — Wikipedia; III Tactical Air Command — Wikipedia; World War II DeRidder Army Air Base Memorial Historical Marker (HMdb).

The 399th Fighter Squadron

The 369th Fighter Group — its 398th, 399th, and 400th Fighter Squadrons — activated August 1, 1943, at Hamilton Field, California, and moved to DeRidder Army Air Base, Louisiana, on March 28, 1944. It was a training group, not a combat unit: through 1944 its pilots flew P-39 Airacobras, A-36s, and P-40s in air-ground maneuvers, including that summer's large-scale Louisiana Maneuvers, before re-equipping with P-51 Mustangs in 1945. The group moved to Stuttgart Army Airfield, Arkansas, on February 8, 1945 — matching Allen's own service record exactly — and was inactivated that August, days after the war's end.

Sources: Air Force Historical Research Agency; Army Air Corps Museum.

“A number of points” — and Fort Dix

The points were the Adjusted Service Rating Score, the War Department’s attempt at an equitable order of discharge, announced on 10 May 1945, two days after the German surrender. A man earned one point for every month of service counted from 16 September 1940, a second point for every month of that service spent overseas, five points for each decoration or campaign participation star, and twelve points for each dependent child up to three; an enlisted man needed eighty-five to be considered for release.

Allen had entered federal service on 16 September 1940, the very day the clock began, had been overseas from January 1942 to the autumn of 1944, and was credited with five campaigns — which on the published rule puts him comfortably above the threshold rather than near it. (That is arithmetic applied to the regulation and his campaign credits, not a figure copied from his papers; the score as actually computed would be in his separation records.) He was discharged at Fort Dix on 8 June 1945, four weeks after the system was announced — a place that would put more than 1.2 million soldiers back into civilian clothes before it was finished with the job. He turned down the reenlistment interview, and got on the train.

Sources: Adjusted Service Rating Score — Wikipedia; Demobilization of United States Armed Forces after World War II — Wikipedia; Fort Dix — GlobalSecurity.org; Fort Dix — Wikipedia.

Three More Attacks, and the One He Refused to Report

The attacks Allen suffered at DeRidder and Stuttgart were not new infections. Plasmodium vivax, the relapsing one of the two New Guinea malarias, leaves dormant parasites in the liver, and neither quinine nor Atabrine could reach them — the drugs suppressed the blood stages only, so relapses typically began about four weeks after a man stopped taking them. Among Allied soldiers with up to a year’s exposure, more than 80 per cent relapsed; in one series of 328 Pacific veterans followed in Minnesota after their suppressive drugs were withdrawn, a quarter of the relapse histories ended within the first year, 53 per cent ran on through the second, and 18 per cent were still going in the third, with a few men relapsing out to four years.

No radical cure existed during the war — primaquine came afterwards — and the Army designated general hospitals in the States as centres for tropical disease, among them Harmon General at Longview, Texas, which also ran a laboratory for imported malaria studies. That is the context for the hardest detail in Part 2: the night before he was to leave for Fort Dix, Allen felt an attack coming and said nothing. The Army had announced its 85-point discharge score on 10 May 1945 and he had the points; a fever reported to the orderly room would have cost him his place in the queue, so he lay down, took the fevers and the chills for an hour and a half, and went to the pictures.

Sources: Plasmodium vivax Relapse Rates in Allied Soldiers during the Second World War: Importance of Hypnozoite Burden, American Journal of Tropical Medicine and Hygiene 107(6), 2022; Demobilization of United States Armed Forces after World War II (Adjusted Service Rating Score, announced 10 May 1945, critical score 85); Preventive Medicine in World War II, Vol. VI, Chapter X: Southwest Pacific Area; Harmon General Hospital, Handbook of Texas, Texas State Historical Association.

The Point System

The “number of points” Allen mentions was the Adjusted Service Rating Score, announced May 10, 1945, two days after Germany's surrender: one point per month of service, one more per month served overseas, five points per battle star or decoration, and twelve points per dependent child (to a maximum of three). Eighty-five points was the initial threshold for discharge eligibility, later lowered to as few as fifty as the Pacific War wound down. Troops called it simply “the point system.”

Source: The National WWII Museum, “The Points Were All That Mattered.”
